# Grease the Groove Method

The Grease the Groove (GTG) method was developed and popularized by Pavel Tsatsouline in *The Naked Warrior* (2003) and is the most direct practical application of the principle that **neurological skill acquisition is separated from muscular fatigue**. The method's premise: if you want to get better at a movement, the most effective strategy is to practice that movement repeatedly in a non-fatigued state rather than accumulating fatigue across long training sessions.

The phrase "grease the groove" refers to the neurological myelination metaphor — each high-quality repetition improves synaptic efficiency along the specific neural pathway used for that movement. Repetitions performed while fatigued use compensated motor patterns and potentially "grease" the wrong groove.

## Core Principle

**Sub-maximal practice at high frequency, never to failure.**

If your maximum rep count is N:
- Each GTG set: 40–60% of N (round down)
- Sessions per day: 2–10 (passive greasing)
- Days per week: 5–7
- Rest between sets: none required — spread throughout the day naturally

## Example Implementation

| Current Pull-up Max | GTG Set Size | Practice Frequency |
|--------------------|--------------|-------------------|
| 5 | 2–3 reps | 5–8 times/day |
| 10 | 4–6 reps | 4–6 times/day |
| 15 | 6–9 reps | 3–5 times/day |
| 20+ | 8–12 reps | 2–4 times/day |

## Who This Is For

- Beginners trying to achieve their first pull-up (start at negatives or band-assisted at 40–60% difficulty)
- Intermediate practitioners trying to double their pull-up max (e.g., from 8 to 15+)
- Athletes who train in a context with built-in natural rest (home workers, office setups with bars installed)
- Anyone on a deload from a structured program who wants to maintain movement frequency without load

## Practical Setup

The method works best when the movement is practiced **opportunistically** rather than as a scheduled workout:

- Install a pull-up bar in a frequently traversed doorway
- Set a push-up station near a work area
- Perform a set each time you pass the station or during natural breaks
- Do not "count" total daily reps — simply practice whenever the opportunity arises

## Interaction With Other Training

GTG is designed to run **alongside** other training, not replace it. Common combinations:

- GTG pull-ups + structured leg/core training (GTG does not interfere with leg recovery)
- GTG push-ups + structured pulling program (the sub-maximal load does not create meaningful recovery demand)

Do **not** attempt GTG on a movement you are currently training to failure in a structured program — the sub-maximal principle requires that the daily sets never approach the fatigue threshold. If GTG sets begin to feel difficult, either reduce rep count or rest a day.

## Performance Testing

**Test maximum performance infrequently** — once per 4–6 weeks. Daily testing destroys the sub-maximal principle and accumulates fatigue, negating the method's mechanism. The improvement in max count tends to be surprising even without frequent testing.

## Key Rules

- **Never train to failure or near failure on any GTG set.** A set where you struggle on the last rep is too heavy. Reduce reps.
- **Technical standard must match competition standard.** Dead-hang pull-ups with full range of motion — not chin-ups, not partial range. The neural pathway being grooved must match the target skill.
- **Spread practice throughout the day, not in a single session.** Clustering all daily GTG sets into one period converts it into a standard training session and reintroduces fatigue-state practice.

> GTG is most effective as a 4–8 week focused block targeting a single movement. After achieving the target, transition to a structured program like `bwf_recommended_routine` or `overcoming_gravity_template` to build balanced strength. Skill specificity: `connective_tissue`, `load_management`.
