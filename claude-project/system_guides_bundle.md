# Bodybuilding Advanced System Guide

## System Definition

**Goal:**
Extract the remaining hypertrophic adaptation available to an athlete near their genetic ceiling by concentrating high-volume overload on one or two muscle groups at a time (specialization blocks), while maintaining all others at minimum volume. At the advanced level, simultaneously training every muscle group at its MAV is physiologically impossible — the combined systemic load exceeds total recovery capacity. The central task is periodizing which muscles receive priority, cycling specialization targets every 6–8 weeks.

**Anti-goals:**
- Not attempting to grow every muscle simultaneously — an advanced athlete's MAV for all muscles combined far exceeds systemic recovery capacity; attempting to train everything at peak volume produces chronic overreaching, not growth
- Not applying intermediate-level linear mesocycle structures — the accumulation phase must be compressed to 3–4 weeks (advanced athletes generate deeper systemic fatigue per session), and deloads must be more comprehensive (50% reduction in both volume AND load)
- Not treating competition prep as an extension of the mass phase — caloric deficit severely constrains recovery capacity; volume must drop to MEV or MV in prep while intensity (load, proximity to failure) is maintained to preserve muscle
- Not chasing new 1RM records during mass phases — high-intensity CNS work competes with the isolated, targeted volume required to sustain MAV-level stimulus on lagging groups

---

## Weekly Structure Logic

| Work Type | Weekly Frequency | Core Reason |
|-----------|-----------------|-------------|
| Priority muscle group (specialization target) | 3–4 focused sessions | Volume must reach MAV–MRV for the target muscle (25–30 sets/week); this requires multiple sessions because session volume >8 sets per muscle is junk volume |
| Maintenance muscle groups | 1 session/group at MV | 2–4 sets per non-target group, 1–2x/week, maintains existing mass during the specialization block without consuming systemic recovery budget |
| High-intensity intraset technique work | 1–2 sessions/week | Myo-reps, rest-pause, and mechanical drop sets are programmed on the priority muscle only; they extend effective reps past failure with minimal additional systemic fatigue |
| Mandatory rest or active recovery | 2–3 days | At advanced loads, joints and tendons are under cumulative stress; 6-day PPL weeks chronically suppress recovery and reduce force quality in subsequent sessions |

**Structural Constraints (hard rules):**

- **Specialization block structure is mandatory: target 1–2 muscle groups per block** — all non-targeted muscles drop to MV (33–50% of previous volume); this frees systemic endocrine and metabolic resources for the priority group
- **Accumulation phase is compressed to 3–4 weeks maximum** — advanced loads generate profound systemic fatigue per session; the MEV–MRV window is narrower than at intermediate level, and accumulated fatigue outpaces adaptation faster
- **Deload depth must increase: 50% reduction in both volume AND intensity** — intermediate deloads require only volume reduction; advanced athletes require full load reduction to clear central CNS fatigue and re-sensitize androgen receptor density
- **Intraset techniques (myo-reps, rest-pause, drop sets) are reserved for the last 1–2 sets of priority muscle work only** — applied to all exercises, these techniques generate excessive systemic stress without proportional hypertrophic benefit
- **Off-season and competition prep are programmed as distinct cycles with different volume-intensity relationships** — running prep-level caloric restriction while maintaining off-season volumes is the fastest route to muscle loss; the two phases cannot overlap

---

## Load Decision Framework

**Off-Season (Mass Phase) load decisions:**
Volume is the primary driver. Track total weekly sets per muscle group; step-load from the bottom of MAV toward MRV across the 3–4 week accumulation block. Increase load when all sets are completed at the top of the target rep range with ≥ 2 RIR; add 1 working set per muscle per week as a secondary progression when load increment is not achievable.

**Competition Prep (Cut Phase) load decisions:**
Volume is reduced to MEV or MV. Intensity is maintained: keep loads at or above those used in the mass phase, and maintain 0–2 RIR on all working sets. The goal is preserving mechanical tension on high-threshold motor units to sustain the protein synthesis signal despite caloric deficit. Reducing load compounds muscle loss by removing both the anabolic stimulus and the caloric surplus simultaneously.

**Specialization block load decisions:**
Priority muscle: train at true 1–3 RIR on all working sets; apply intraset techniques (myo-reps, rest-pause) on 1–2 sets per session to extend effective reps. Non-priority muscles: 2–4 sets at 3–4 RIR; load maintained but volume aggressively reduced.

**When to rotate the specialization target:**
End the specialization block at 6–8 weeks and rotate to a new target. Signals to end early: logbook stagnation on the priority muscle persisting across 2 sessions despite deload; accumulated joint aching in the priority muscle group; systemic recovery indicators declining despite adherence to the deload schedule.

**Intraset technique decision rules:**
- **Myo-reps:** Activation set of 12–15 reps to 1–2 RIR, followed by 3–5 mini-sets of 3–5 reps with 15-second rest. Use when the goal is equivalent hypertrophy with 30% less volume-load — useful when specialization target is already at MRV.
- **Rest-pause:** Set to volitional concentric failure, 15–20 second rest, then 1–2 more mini-sets to failure. Use when extending intraset time-under-tension is the priority.
- **Drop sets:** Complete set to failure, reduce load 20–30%, continue to failure. Use primarily for isolation exercises on priority muscles; systemically expensive on compound movements.
- **Mechanical drop sets:** Failure in a mechanically weak position (e.g., incline fly), immediately switch to a mechanically stronger variant (e.g., incline press) with the same load. Use to extend set duration past failure without load reduction.

---

## Movement Category Definitions

### Specialization Block Strategy

At the advanced level, the movement category framework is applied differently than at intermediate level. Rather than distributing volume across all categories equally across all weeks, advanced programming concentrates volume on 1–2 movement categories (the target muscles) while reducing all others to maintenance.

**Example specialization rotation:**
- Block 1 (6–8 weeks): Lateral deltoid + biceps priority; all other muscles at MV
- Block 2 (6–8 weeks): Quad + hamstring priority; all other muscles at MV
- Block 3 (6–8 weeks): Back width (lat) + chest priority; all other muscles at MV

### Stretch-Mediated Hypertrophy Integration

At the advanced level, exercise selection must prioritize loaded stretch for every priority muscle:

| Priority Muscle | Best Lengthened-Position Exercise | Why |
|-----------------|----------------------------------|-----|
| Triceps long head | Overhead cable extension | Shoulder flexion stretches the biarticular long head over the glenohumeral joint |
| Biceps long head | Incline dumbbell curl | Shoulder hyperextension in the bottom position stretches the long head at its origin |
| Lateral deltoid | Cable lateral raise (arm at side) | Full ROM from fully adducted to 90° — cable provides continuous tension unlike dumbbell |
| Hamstrings | Seated leg curl, RDL | Hip flexed under load places biarticular hamstrings at full length |
| Gastrocnemius | Standing calf raise (straight leg) | Knee extension prevents active insufficiency; maximal stretch at full dorsiflexion |

**Lengthened partials:** As an intraset technique past concentric failure, perform partial reps strictly in the deep stretched range. Evidence supports that lengthened partials post-failure provide an additional stretch-mediated hypertrophic stimulus beyond full-ROM reps.

### Advanced Technique Application by Category

**Horizontal push:** Myo-reps on flat cable fly (peak contraction stimulus); rest-pause on flat dumbbell press (maintains load while extending volume).

**Vertical pull:** Mechanical drop set from wide-grip pulldown (mechanically weak) to neutral-grip pulldown (stronger position) using same stack weight.

**Elbow flexion:** Myo-reps on incline dumbbell curl are highly effective — the activation set provides stretch-mediated stimulus; mini-sets provide cumulative mechanical tension.

**Elbow extension:** Overhead cable extension as priority set; cable pushdown as drop-set continuation when overhead position fatigues (mechanical drop set by position change).

## Milestones

### Technical Indicators

- All intraset techniques (myo-reps, rest-pause) executed with controlled form; no technical breakdown on extension sets
- Accurate identification of true concentric failure vs. technical failure vs. psychological failure during set
- Peak week implementation: structured carbohydrate cycling (depletion Day 1–3 at ~1g/kg, loading Day 4–5 at 5–8g/kg) executed without under- or over-shooting

### Competition Prep Indicators

- Maintained all primary working loads at or above off-season levels within the final 4 weeks of prep
- Body weight tracking shows ≤ 0.5 kg/week loss rate (faster indicates muscle loss alongside fat loss)
- Strength in all primary compound movements held within 5% of off-season levels at the end of prep

**Signal that advanced strategies are exhausted:**
Annual lean mass gain <0.1 kg despite optimal nutrition, periodization, and pharmacological-free status; this is the genetic ceiling boundary and beyond the scope of any programming manipulation.

---

## Common Violations

| Violation | Root Cause | Correct Approach |
|-----------|-----------|-----------------|
| Training every muscle at MAV simultaneously | Applying intermediate structure to an advanced recovery budget | The combined MAV of all muscles exceeds systemic recovery capacity; specialization blocks rotate priority, allowing local overload with systemic balance |
| Maintaining off-season volume during caloric deficit prep | Assuming recovery capacity is diet-independent | Energy availability is the primary limiter of recovery during prep; volume must drop to MEV/MV while intensity is preserved to prevent muscle loss |
| Applying intraset techniques to all exercises in all sessions | Believing more technique intensity always means more growth | Myo-reps and rest-pause techniques generate high systemic fatigue when applied broadly; their benefit is targeted extension of effective reps on priority muscles only |
| 6-day PPL every week year-round | Equating daily training frequency with dedication | Chronic 6-day training suppresses CNS recovery and joint repair capacity; 4–5 days with dedicated rest produces superior long-term quality in advanced athletes |
| Water restriction in peak week | Folklore: "drying out" produces definition | Muscle tissue is 70–75% water; restricting water flattens muscle fullness and prevents glycogen from binding; continuous hydration with structured carb cycling is the evidence-based protocol |
| Sodium depletion in peak week | Folklore: sodium causes subcutaneous water retention | Sodium restriction spikes aldosterone, causing kidneys to reabsorb water into the interstitial space, producing bloating rather than dryness; keep sodium stable until 2 hours before stage |
| Deloading only volume without load reduction | Applying intermediate deload protocols to advanced fatigue | At advanced loads, CNS fatigue accumulates from both volume and intensity; halving only volume while maintaining heavy loads does not clear central fatigue; both must be reduced by 50% |

# Bodybuilding Intermediate System Guide

## System Definition

**Goal:**
Build lean muscle mass systematically by controlling weekly volume per muscle group within the MEV–MAV range, training with genuine proximity to failure (1–3 RIR on working sets), and progressing load or reps across every mesocycle. The central task at the intermediate level is transitioning away from session-to-session linear loading — which stops working as absolute loads generate fatigue that cannot clear in 48 hours — toward mesocyclic volume periodization with autoregulated intensity.

**Anti-goals:**
- Not chasing strength peaks or 1RM PRs — the primary variable is lean tissue accumulation, not barbell numbers; grinding near-maximal singles limits the volume capacity required for hypertrophy
- Not prioritizing exercise variety as a training goal — changing exercises every session ("muscle confusion") keeps the body in perpetual motor learning mode, diverting resources from protein accretion to pattern repair
- Not relying exclusively on compound movements for all muscle groups — the lateral deltoid, biceps long head, and triceps long head are systematically under-stimulated by pressing and pulling patterns; isolation is required, not optional
- Not treating deloads as optional — accumulated fatigue at the intermediate level manifests as logbook stagnation before it manifests as subjective tiredness; waiting until you feel exhausted means weeks of junk volume have already passed

---

## Weekly Structure Logic

| Work Type | Weekly Frequency | Core Reason |
|-----------|-----------------|-------------|
| Horizontal push (chest emphasis) | 2 days | Pec major responds poorly to 1x/week at intermediate volume loads; 2x distributes sets below the 8/session diminishing-return threshold |
| Vertical pull (lat emphasis) | 2 days | Frequency is the primary driver of lat sweep at intermediate level; 1x/week leaves insufficient stimulus density |
| Vertical push (shoulder) | 2 days | Lateral and posterior deltoid MEV is high (8 sets/week); single sessions cannot reach that volume without quality degradation |
| Horizontal pull (upper back thickness) | 2 days | Mid-trap and rhomboid development requires frequency to match the volume from bilateral compound rows |
| Elbow flexion isolation (biceps) | 2 days | Compound pulling trains brachialis primarily; biceps long head requires supinated curl volume separate from row volume |
| Elbow extension isolation (triceps) | 2 days | Triceps long head requires overhead position for stretch-loaded stimulus; pressing alone does not provide this |
| Knee extension (quad isolation) | 2 days | Squatting develops the vasti adequately only if quad MRV is not approached; most intermediates need leg extension to close the quad volume gap |
| Knee flexion (hamstring isolation) | 2 days | The seated leg curl uniquely loads the hamstrings in the stretched, hip-flexed position — a stimulus compound hinges cannot replicate |
| Hip extension (glute/posterior chain) | 2 days | Hip thrusts and RDLs target glute maximum at a moment arm angle that squats do not reach |
| Mandatory rest | 2–3 days | At the intermediate level, joint connective tissue adapts slower than muscle; two consecutive push or pull days compound tendon load before adequate repair |

**Structural Constraints (hard rules):**

- **No muscle group trained on consecutive days** — 48 hours of recovery is the minimum SRA window for intermediate lifters at intermediate volumes; back-to-back sessions for the same group generate fatigue faster than adaptation
- **Proximity to failure is mandatory on working sets: 1–3 RIR** — sets stopped at 5+ RIR fail to recruit high-threshold motor units; the high-threshold type II fibers that have the greatest growth capacity are only recruited as peripheral fatigue forces the CNS to call them in; stopping early means they never receive mechanical tension
- **Maximum 8 working sets per muscle per session** — beyond 8 sets, hypertrophic efficiency per set plateaus while fatigue accumulates linearly; additional sets at session volumes >8 are junk volume
- **Volume is stepped across the mesocycle: MEV → MAV → deload** — flat weekly volume produces no progressive overload stimulus; the target is Week 1 at ~10–12 sets/muscle, adding 1–2 sets/muscle/week for 4–5 weeks, deloading to MV in Week 6
- **Exercise selection is stable within a mesocycle** — changing the exercise resets the motor pattern and produces DOMS-driven protein repair demand, competing with hypertrophic accretion; rotate exercises between mesocycles, not within them

---

## Load Decision Framework

**When to increase load (double progression):**
Work within a target rep range (e.g., 8–12). When all prescribed sets are completed at the top of the range (12 reps) with ≥ 2 RIR remaining on the final set, increase load at the next session and reset reps to the bottom of the range (8 reps). This is the primary overload mechanism for intermediate bodybuilders.

**When to increase volume instead of load:**
If a load increase would drop below the target rep range, add one working set per muscle group instead. Volume progression is the secondary overload mechanism when load progression stalls mid-mesocycle.

**When to deload (signal-based, not calendar-based):**
Initiate a deload week when two or more of the following appear across three consecutive sessions:
- Logbook stagnation or regression: reps declining at the same load despite adequate sleep
- Persistent DOMS beyond 72 hours (muscle is not recovering before the next session)
- Submaximal warm-up sets rating 1–2 RPE points higher than baseline
- Sleep quality deterioration or elevated resting heart rate
- Connective tissue aching that worsens during, not before, the session

Deload to MV (4–6 sets/muscle/week) for one week, maintaining load but eliminating volume.

**Autoregulation (RIR vs. fixed percentages):**
Fixed percentage loading fails at the intermediate level because daily performance fluctuates by 5–10% based on sleep, stress, and nutrition. Prescribing intensity as "complete at 2 RIR" allows absolute load to match biological readiness daily, ensuring each set lands in the productive 1–3 RIR window regardless of day-to-day variability.

## Movement Category Definitions

### Horizontal Push (Chest Emphasis)

- **Why this system requires this category:** The pectoralis major sternal and costal heads require loaded horizontal adduction across their full range to receive the stretch-mediated and peak-tension hypertrophic stimulus that incline pressing alone cannot provide.
- **What this typically means in this system:** Flat dumbbell press (primary — allows full pec stretch at the bottom), flat cable fly (peak tension in the shortened position), hammer-strength chest press
- **Selection criteria:** Elbows at 45–60° of flare to align load with chest fiber direction; scapulae retracted and depressed throughout; full ROM from stretched bottom to adducted top
- **Why dumbbells over barbells for hypertrophy:** Barbells limit ROM at the bottom; dumbbells allow the hands to drop below chest level, loading the pec in its fully stretched position where stretch-mediated hypertrophy signal is highest
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 2 days
- **Volume range:** 10–20 sets/week across mesocycle
- **Intensity range:** 8–15 RM; avoid <6 RM (unnecessary axial loading, limited hypertrophic volume)

---

### Incline Push (Upper Chest and Anterior Deltoid)

- **Why this system requires this category:** The clavicular head of the pectoralis major has a distinct fiber direction (horizontal–upward) that flat pressing cannot optimally load; it requires an incline vector.
- **What this typically means in this system:** 30° incline dumbbell press (primary), incline cable press or Smith machine incline
- **Selection criteria:** Restrict incline to 30° — beyond 45°, the anterior deltoid dominates and pec recruitment drops; the 30° angle maximizes clavicular pec tension while keeping deltoid contribution secondary
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 2 days (often programmed on the same day as flat pressing)
- **Volume range:** 8–16 sets/week

---

### Vertical Push (Shoulder Emphasis)

- **Why this system requires this category:** Lateral deltoid MEV is 8 sets/week and MRV is 26+ sets/week — far exceeding what incidental shoulder loading during pressing provides. Direct lateral raise volume is the primary driver of shoulder width.
- **What this typically means in this system:** Seated dumbbell lateral raise (primary compound), cable lateral raise (continuous tension through the ROM), rear delt cable fly or reverse pec-deck
- **Selection criteria for overhead pressing:** Press in the scapular plane (30° tuck) to protect glenohumeral joint integrity; seated dumbbell or Smith press preferred over barbell overhead for better ROM control
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 2 days
- **Volume range:** Lateral delt: 12–22 sets/week; posterior delt: 10–18 sets/week

---

### Vertical Pull (Lat Emphasis)

- **Why this system requires this category:** Lat width ("V-taper") is developed through vertical pulling in the shoulder extension plane. Horizontal rows develop thickness but cannot replace the lat sweep stimulus of pull-down patterns.
- **What this typically means in this system:** Neutral-grip lat pulldown (primary — allows full overhead shoulder flexion without impingement), single-arm cable row (lat isolation), pull-ups or weighted pull-ups
- **Selection criteria:** Drive elbows toward the iliac crest, not behind the torso; prevent shoulder hyperextension at the bottom; full dead-hang starting position to train the lat in its lengthened state
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 2 days
- **Volume range:** 12–22 sets/week

---

### Horizontal Pull (Upper Back Thickness)

- **Why this system requires this category:** Mid-trap, rhomboids, and posterior deltoid — the muscles that create back thickness in lateral view — are trained in the horizontal pulling plane. Vertical pulls cannot load scapular retraction adequately.
- **What this typically means in this system:** Chest-supported T-bar row or machine row (primary — removes spinal erector demand, isolating upper back), seated cable row with wide grip (scapular retraction emphasis)
- **Selection criteria:** Allow full scapular protraction under load at the start; actively retract at peak concentric range; pause at contraction to eliminate momentum
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 2 days
- **Volume range:** 10–18 sets/week

---

### Elbow Flexion (Biceps)

- **Why this system requires this category:** Vertical and horizontal pulling exercises primarily load the brachialis and brachioradialis; the biceps brachii long head is selectively loaded by supinated curls, particularly in the shoulder-hyperextended position (incline curl) where it is lengthened at the origin.
- **What this typically means in this system:** Incline dumbbell curl (long head stretch loading), preacher curl or cable curl (peak tension in the shortened position), EZ-bar or barbell curl
- **Selection criteria:** Anchor elbows in space — prevent shoulder flexion, which recruits anterior deltoid and reduces biceps tension; supinated grip throughout for biceps brachii vs. neutral grip for brachialis emphasis
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 2 days
- **Volume range:** 12–20 sets/week

---

### Elbow Extension (Triceps)

- **Why this system requires this category:** The triceps long head is biarticular — it crosses the shoulder joint. Pressing movements keep it in a shortened position, providing poor length-tension stimulus. Overhead extensions place the long head in a fully stretched state, providing the stretch-mediated hypertrophic signal that pushdowns cannot.
- **What this typically means in this system:** Overhead cable extension or EZ-bar overhead extension (long head primary), cable pushdown or reverse-grip pushdown (lateral and medial heads)
- **Selection criteria:** Maintain strict elbow position; prevent scapular elevation or shoulder movement; the only joint moving is the elbow
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 2 days
- **Volume range:** 10–16 sets/week

---

### Knee Extension (Quad Isolation)

- **Why this system requires this category:** Squats and leg press train the vasti through a compound pattern with the hip extensors sharing the load. Isolated knee extension loads the rectus femoris and vasti directly in the knee extension plane, and the seated leg extension is the only exercise that provides significant peak tension in the quad's shortened position.
- **What this typically means in this system:** Seated leg extension (primary), hack squat or sissy squat (rectus femoris stretch loading)
- **Selection criteria:** Control the eccentric transition to prevent patellar tendon strain at the peak knee flexion position; full ROM from terminal flexion to full extension
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 2 days
- **Volume range:** 10–18 sets/week (combined with compound quad work)

---

### Knee Flexion (Hamstring Isolation)

- **Why this system requires this category:** The seated leg curl is uniquely capable of loading the hamstrings with the hip flexed, placing them in their fully lengthened position — a stimulus that Romanian deadlifts and standing curls cannot replicate fully.
- **What this typically means in this system:** Seated leg curl (primary — hip-flexed position loads hamstrings at full length), lying leg curl (secondary)
- **Selection criteria:** Secure the pelvis into the pad to prevent anterior pelvic tilt, which shortens the hamstrings at their origin and reduces the stretch stimulus; full ROM
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 2 days
- **Volume range:** 10–16 sets/week

---

### Hip Extension (Glute and Posterior Chain)

- **Why this system requires this category:** The gluteus maximus is maximally active during exercises that combine hip hyperextension with knee stability — specifically the hip thrust. Squats and deadlifts do not provide this positioning.
- **What this typically means in this system:** Barbell hip thrust (primary), Romanian deadlift (hip hinge with hamstring stretch loading), cable glute kickback
- **Selection criteria:** Initiate via a pure hip hinge on RDLs; avoid lumbar hyperextension at the top of hip thrusts; maintain a neutral spine throughout
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 2 days
- **Volume range:** 8–16 sets/week for glutes

---

## Milestones

### Technical Indicators

- Consistent 2 RIR execution: able to accurately estimate RIR on main working sets across all movement categories (verified by rep completion matching predicted RIR)
- Controlled eccentric tempo (2–3 seconds) on all isolation movements without momentum or bouncing
- All major compound movements (bench, squat, deadlift, OHP) executed with stable technique under working loads — not just empty-bar form

### Strength / Fitness Indicators

These are approximate standards for a natural, male intermediate bodybuilder (~80 kg bodyweight):

| Exercise | Standard |
|----------|----------|
| Bench press | 100 kg 1RM (1.25× BW) |
| Barbell squat | 125–140 kg 1RM (1.5–1.75× BW) |
| Deadlift | 160 kg 1RM (2.0× BW) |
| Overhead press | 58–64 kg 1RM (0.70–0.80× BW) |
| Weighted pull-up | +20–40 kg for 5 reps |

**Signal to advance to Advanced level:**
Genuine stagnation across two or more consecutive mesocycles despite optimal nutrition and recovery; MEV for most muscle groups has elevated to near-MRV levels; lean mass gains have slowed to <0.5 kg/year under consistent training; the athlete has exhausted double-progression on all primary exercises.

## Common Violations

| Violation | Root Cause | Correct Approach |
|-----------|-----------|-----------------|
| Training with 5+ RIR on working sets ("comfortable hard") | Conflating fatigue with proximity to failure | High-threshold motor units are only recruited as peripheral fatigue forces CNS recruitment; sets stopped at 5+ RIR never recruit the fibers with the greatest growth capacity |
| Adding sets without maintaining 1–3 RIR | Believing more sets always means more growth | 15 sets at 5 RIR generates high systemic fatigue while failing to stimulate high-threshold motor units; this produces the "high fatigue, low stimulus" state that causes non-functional overreaching |
| Changing exercises every session or every week | Chasing novelty under the "muscle confusion" myth | Novel eccentric stress from new exercises triggers excessive DOMS and protein repair demand that competes with hypertrophic accretion; rotate exercises between mesocycles, not within them |
| Skipping isolation work for lateral delts and biceps | Assuming compound movements cover everything | The lateral deltoid is barely loaded during overhead pressing; the biceps long head requires supinated curl volume separate from rowing; these muscles will not develop without direct work |
| Ignoring lengthened-position exercises | Not knowing about stretch-mediated hypertrophy | Titin mechanosensing under loaded stretch drives sarcomerogenesis; training only in shortened ranges (pushdowns over overhead extensions, standing over seated leg curls) loses this non-overlapping stimulus |
| Skipping deloads because "I don't feel overtrained" | Waiting for subjective fatigue rather than logbook signals | Logbook stagnation precedes subjective overtraining by weeks; by the time you feel overtrained, you have already spent weeks accumulating fatigue beyond MRV |

# Calisthenics Advanced System Guide

## System Definition

**Goal:**
Develop advanced static strength skills — straddle planche, straddle front lever, and freestanding handstand push-up — alongside elite dynamic skills (weighted ring muscle-up, one-arm pull-up progression) through daily skill practice with structured strength support. The defining characteristic of advanced calisthenics is that the primary adaptation target is not muscle or strength in a general sense, but the specific force production capacity at precise joint angles in extended lever positions. No other training system demands this level of positional specificity.

**Anti-goals:**
- Not developing general strength or fitness — barbell lifts, running, and metabolic conditioning are irrelevant to advanced calisthenics skill development and compete for recovery resources
- Not maximizing muscle hypertrophy — excess bodyweight directly increases the leverage demands of planche and front lever; bodyweight management is a competitive variable, not an afterthought
- Not rushing progression steps — the connective tissue adaptation timeline for planche and front lever positions is 6–18 months at minimum; athletes who advance steps before tendons have adapted accumulate stress that manifests as elbow, shoulder, and wrist injuries weeks later
- Not training skills to failure — at the advanced level, the movements are complex enough that a single technically failed rep can establish a compensatory motor pattern that takes weeks to unlearn

---

## Weekly Structure Logic

| Work Type | Weekly Frequency | Core Reason |
|-----------|-----------------|-------------|
| Planche progressions (skill practice) | 3–4 days | Planche adaptation requires high frequency; each session must be at low fatigue |
| Front lever progressions (skill practice) | 3 days | Front lever and planche can share sessions when both are at manageable progression steps |
| Handstand / HSPU progressions | 3 days | Overhead balance and pressing are separate skill families from planche/front lever |
| Ring dynamics (weighted muscle-up, ring strength) | 3 days | Ring work maintains the shoulder stability and pressing capacity that supports all other skills |
| Weighted pull-ups (strength support) | 3 days | Primary strength driver for front lever and one-arm pull-up development |
| Core tension (hollow body, advanced L-sit, V-sit) | Every session | Hollow body is the active position in every planche and front lever hold; must be automatic |

**Structural Constraints (hard rules):**

- **Scapular activation is performed every single training day without exception** — 3 × 15 scapular pull-ups before any pulling or planche work; at the advanced level, the volume of isometric shoulder loading is sufficient to cause chronic impingement without consistent stabilizer preparation
- **Planche and front lever are never trained to technical failure** — define the stopping condition before starting (e.g., "stop when hips drop more than 5 cm") and enforce it; at advanced loading levels, a single degraded hold can cause acute tendon stress
- **Planche (push / scapular protraction) and front lever (pull / scapular depression) are biomechanical antagonists and can share the same session** — always sequence planche first (higher CNS demand and greater anterior elbow tendon load), front lever second; what must be avoided is back-to-back full-intensity sessions where both skills are simultaneously at their maximal progression step; the actual constraint is total elbow flexor load per microcycle, not per-session separation; one day of lower-intensity maintenance on one skill while the other recovers is acceptable scheduling
- **Gelatin + Vitamin C protocol is mandatory before every session** — not optional; this applies especially to planche, front lever, and ring work; Shaw et al. 2017 RCT (*Am J Clin Nutr*, DOI: 10.3945/ajcn.116.138594, n=8): 15 g gelatin + ~48 mg Vitamin C, 60 minutes pre-training, doubles circulating collagen precursors and PINP; Lee et al. 2024 (*J Nutr*, RCT) shows 30 g hydrolyzed collagen + 50 mg Vit C produces a greater dose-response increase in collagen synthesis after resistance training; see `connective_tissue` for full protocol
- **New progression steps are introduced only after the current step meets the hold standard across 3 consecutive sessions** — one exceptional session does not justify advancement; consistency across sessions is the criterion

---

## Load Decision Framework

**Static skill progression standard:**
Progress to the next step in the planche or front lever ladder when the current hold is sustained for 3 × 8–10 seconds with ≥ 2 seconds of perceptible reserve across 3 consecutive training sessions. "Perceptible reserve" means the position did not degrade at the end of the hold; the athlete stopped by choice, not by failure.

**Dynamic skill progression standard:**
Progress when 3 × 3 clean reps are achievable at the current step — for ring muscle-up variants, planche push-ups, front lever rows, etc.

**When to regress:**
- Pain in any of: medial elbow, lateral elbow, wrist (dorsal or volar), or anterior shoulder → immediately drop two progression steps and apply connective tissue protocol; do not train through
- Hold quality has deteriorated at the same progression step over two consecutive sessions → reduce session volume by 30% before adjusting the progression step; fatigue is the more likely cause
- The current step cannot be maintained for more than 4 seconds per hold → the previous step was not fully consolidated; return to it

**Planche lean angle objectivity:**
Proprioception systematically underestimates forward lean — athletes consistently feel "very far forward" while video shows only a modest lean angle, and the pelvis posteriorly tilts as compensation without the athlete noticing. Use a fixed floor reference (a tape line aligned with the middle finger, against which the shoulder must extend measurably forward) to make lean angle a measurable variable rather than a body-feel estimate. Alternatively, record every planche session from a lateral angle with the wrist, shoulder, and hip visible in the same frame; lean angle can then be objectively tracked session over session.

**The overreaching trap specific to static skills:**
Advanced static skills feel deceptively manageable in the first 2–3 weeks of a new progression step because the muscles can produce the required force while the tendons have not yet been stressed. The injury window is weeks 3–6: muscles are adapting quickly, tendons are beginning to accumulate stress, and the perceived difficulty remains low. Reduce volume at week 4 proactively, regardless of how easy the holds feel.

---

## Movement Category Definitions

### Planche Progressions

- **What this system requires:** Force production in a horizontal position where the entire body is parallel to the ground and supported only by the hands. This requires simultaneous scapular protraction, shoulder depression, elbow extension, core anti-extension, and hip extension — none of which can be isolated and trained separately with the same effect.
- **Progression ladder (each step requires 3 × 8–10 s hold across 3 sessions before advancing):**
  Tuck planche (knees to chest) → Advanced tuck (back flat, knees at hip level) → Straddle (legs apart, straight) → Full planche (legs together)
- **The lean angle principle:** Planche difficulty is determined by the horizontal displacement of the center of mass in front of the hands. Lean angle (how far forward the shoulders are from the wrists) is the primary programming variable. Increase lean angle before advancing the leg position.
- **What distinguishes planche from other pressing:** The planche demands scapular protraction under extreme load — the opposite of the scapular retraction cued in most pushing exercises. Athletes with a background in retraction-heavy training (powerlifting, barbell pressing) must actively re-pattern this before planche training is productive.
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 3–4 days (each session: 5–8 working holds; never more)
- **Volume cap:** No more than 8 working holds per session at any progression step; more is not better and accumulates tendon stress without proportional adaptation

---

### Front Lever Progressions

- **What this system requires:** Full-body horizontal suspension from a bar or rings with the body parallel to the ground, requiring isometric force from the lats, lower trapezius, posterior chain, and anti-flexion core simultaneously.
- **Progression ladder (same standard as planche — 3 × 8–10 s across 3 sessions):**
  Tuck front lever → Advanced tuck (back flat) → One-leg extended → Straddle → Full front lever
- **Front lever rows as strength support:** Front lever rows (pulling from the front lever position to a tuck or inverted row) develop the pulling strength needed to maintain the full position; they are not the same as training the hold itself and should be treated as separate exercises
- **Interaction with planche training:** Planche and front lever both load the shoulder complex and elbow tendons in extended positions. When both are in the program simultaneously, they must be separated across days or sequenced with planche first (higher CNS demand) and front lever second in the same session.
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 3 days
- **Volume cap:** 6–8 working holds per session

---

### Handstand and Freestanding HSPU

- **What this system requires:** Overhead balance, wrist stability, shoulder elevation strength under load, and the motor control to maintain alignment while pressing in a freestanding inverted position.
- **The handstand and the HSPU are separate skills:** A 60-second freestanding handstand does not guarantee a single freestanding HSPU; the pressing strength and the balance skill are both required simultaneously. Train them separately before combining.
- **HSPU progression ladder:**
  Elevated pike push-up (feet on box) → Wall HSPU (head to pad, then head to floor) → Wall HSPU with deficit (hands on parallettes) → Freestanding HSPU negative → Freestanding HSPU
- **Handstand pressing constraint:** In freestanding HSPU, the press must be vertical — any horizontal drift during the pressing phase means balance is being compensated rather than maintained. An HSPU that completes but with a visible forward drift has not met the technical standard.
- **Wrist management:** HSPU and planche both load the wrist in extreme extension. These two categories should not both be trained in a fatigued wrist state on the same day; manage total wrist extension loading across the week.
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 3 days (handstand practice daily is acceptable if volume is low; HSPU strength work 3 days)

---

### Ring Dynamics (Weighted Muscle-Up and Ring Strength)

- **What this system requires:** The ring muscle-up at the advanced level is a platform for loaded progressions (weighted muscle-up, ring muscle-up to L-sit, ring muscle-up to support hold). Ring strength also maintains the shoulder stability foundation that supports all planche and front lever work.
- **The rings-out position in advanced context:** At the advanced level, the rings-out (RTO) position is the default for all ring support work. Every ring dip, every support hold, every transition should end with fully turned-out rings. This is no longer a cue — it is the standard.
- **Advanced ring muscle-up variants (progression order):**
  Strict ring muscle-up → Weighted ring muscle-up (+5 kg → +10 kg) → Ring muscle-up to L-sit → Ring muscle-up to support + hold → Slow (3-second) ring muscle-up
- **Interaction with planche:** Ring support holds and planche both require scapular depression and protraction under load. Sequencing them in the same session should place ring work after planche, as planche has higher CNS demand.
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 3 days

---

### Weighted Pull-Ups (Front Lever and One-Arm Strength Base)

- **What this system requires:** Maximal pulling strength is the primary limiter for both the full front lever and one-arm pull-up progressions. An athlete who cannot complete a weighted pull-up at +50% bodyweight will not achieve a full front lever regardless of how many holds are practiced.
- **The one-arm pull-up progression ladder:**
  Archer pull-up (wide base) → Assisted one-arm (resistance band at wrist) → One-arm negative (5-second lowering) → Partial one-arm (chin-level only) → Full one-arm pull-up
- **Load target for front lever:** Weighted pull-up at +50% bodyweight for 3 reps is the minimum strength prerequisite for a full front lever attempt; below this, strength — not hold practice — is the bottleneck.
- **Total elbow flexor loading cap (OAP + Front Lever):** One-arm pull-up negatives impose extreme eccentric stress on the distal biceps tendon and medial epicondyle; front lever holds impose sustained isometric stress on the same structures. When both are in the program in the same microcycle, their volumes must be managed as a single shared budget — not planned independently. If front lever is in a volume-building week, OAP work is in maintenance mode (holds only, no negatives); if OAP is receiving progressive overload, front lever volume is held constant. Escalating both simultaneously within the same 7-day window is the fastest path to medial elbow injury in advanced calisthenics.
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 3 days
- **Intensity range:** 3–5 RM with added weight

---

## Milestones

### Technical Indicators

- Straddle planche hold: 3–5 continuous seconds with flat back, depressed shoulders, locked elbows, and both legs straight at shoulder width or wider
- Straddle front lever: 5 continuous seconds with body parallel to ground and arms locked
- Freestanding handstand: 30–45 continuous seconds with clean thoracic-lumbar alignment and no balance compensation requiring arm-to-side adjustment
- Weighted ring muscle-up: 3 reps at +10 kg with strict technique and full RTO at lockout

### Strength / Fitness Indicators

- Weighted pull-up: +50% bodyweight for 3 reps
- Ring dips (weighted): +20 kg for 5 reps
- Hollow body hold: 60 continuous seconds
- L-sit on parallettes: 15 continuous seconds (full leg extension, hips at handle height)

**Signal of reaching the top of the advanced tier:**
Straddle planche 5-second hold, straddle front lever 10-second hold, and freestanding HSPU for 3 clean reps achieved simultaneously — not sequentially across months, but maintainable in the same training phase.

---

## Common Violations

| Violation | Root Cause | Correct Approach |
|-----------|-----------|-----------------|
| Advancing planche or front lever steps after one successful session | Confusing a single successful performance with established skill | Three consecutive sessions at the hold standard is the criterion; one exceptional day is a data point, not a progression trigger |
| Skipping the gelatin + Vitamin C pre-training protocol | Viewing it as optional supplementation rather than connective tissue maintenance | At advanced planche and front lever loading, this protocol is the primary connective tissue protection mechanism available; see `connective_tissue` |
| Escalating volume on both planche and front lever simultaneously within the same microcycle | Treating biomechanical antagonists as independent training recovery budgets | Manage them under a single shared elbow flexor capacity budget; if one pushes progression, the other holds constant |
| Continuing after the first sign of medial elbow pain | Misclassifying golfer's elbow as soreness | Medial elbow pain during planche or front lever work is the most common acute injury signal in advanced calisthenics; drop two progression steps immediately and apply connective tissue protocol |
| Training freestanding HSPU without first achieving a 60-second handstand hold | Attempting to combine pressing strength and balance before either is established | The freestanding HSPU requires both components simultaneously; attempt it only after the handstand hold is stable and wall HSPU pressing strength is established |
| Adding planche volume to compensate for slow progress | Believing that more holds produce faster adaptation | Connective tissue adaptation rate is fixed by biology; doubling the hold volume does not double the adaptation rate, but does double the injury risk; patience is the correct response to slow progress, not volume increases |

# Calisthenics Intermediate System Guide

## System Definition

**Goal:**
Acquire the foundational skill set of intermediate calisthenics — ring muscle-up, wall handstand push-up, pistol squat, and L-sit — by structuring training around deliberate skill practice on a fresh nervous system, supported by dedicated strength accumulation sessions. The central principle is that skill acquisition and strength accumulation operate on different timescales and must be trained under different fatigue conditions. Confusing the two is the most common structural error at this level.

**Anti-goals:**
- Not maximizing hypertrophy — muscle mass that cannot be expressed through controlled bodyweight movement is not useful in this system; aesthetics is a byproduct, not a target
- Not chasing absolute load numbers — there is no meaningful barbell 1RM equivalent; progress is measured by skill progression milestones and weighted movement capacity, not a single number
- Not training skills to failure — a failed skill attempt reinforces an incorrect motor pattern; stopping 2 reps before form breaks is not conservatism, it is the correct training protocol
- Not substituting volume for quality — 10 perfect ring muscle-up attempts produce more adaptation than 30 degraded attempts; volume accumulation applies to strength work, not skill practice

---

## Weekly Structure Logic

| Work Type | Weekly Frequency | Core Reason |
|-----------|-----------------|-------------|
| Dedicated skill practice (lowest fatigue state of the week) | 1 day | Skills require the clearest neuromuscular signal available; this session produces no meaningful strength fatigue |
| Pull strength (weighted pull-ups, scapular work) | 2 days | Primary strength driver for muscle-up and front lever development |
| Push strength (ring dips, parallel bar dips) | 2 days | Primary strength driver for muscle-up and planche foundation |
| Horizontal push/pull (archer push-ups, inverted rows) | 2 days | Unilateral strength development; bridges toward one-arm progressions |
| Core tension (hollow body, L-sit progressions, Pallof press) | 3–4 days | Hollow body is the structural foundation for all kipping and static skill movements |
| Overhead pressing / handstand pathway (pike push-up → wall HSPU) | 2–3 days | HSPU milestone requires a dedicated vertical pressing progression; ring push strength does not transfer to overhead pressing mechanics |
| Unilateral lower body (pistol squat progressions) | 2 days | Pistol squat milestone requires its own progression ladder; bilateral leg work provides insufficient specificity |

**Structural Constraints (hard rules):**

- **Skill practice belongs on the day immediately following a complete rest day** — CNS fatigue and connective tissue micro-stress from heavy loading peak 24–48 hours after heavy sessions; placing skill practice after two consecutive strength days (e.g., Wednesday after Monday and Tuesday heavy pulling and pressing) means the nervous system is at its worst precisely when it needs to be at its best; the correct structure is: complete rest day → skill practice day → strength days; if schedule constraints prevent a standalone skill day, distribute skill practice to the first 15–20 minutes of every training session before any strength work begins — this is always better than a standalone skill session that falls mid-fatigue-block
- **Skill practice precedes all strength work within any shared session** — if skills and strength must share a day, skills come first, always; strength work under skill-practice fatigue is acceptable; skill practice under strength fatigue is not
- **Scapular activation is non-negotiable before every pull session** — 3 sets of scapular pull-ups before any weighted or skill pulling work; this is not warm-up, it is injury prevention infrastructure for athletes doing high-volume ring and bar pulling
- **Never train a skill to technical failure** — define a stopping condition before beginning each skill set (e.g., "stop if the transition loses control") and enforce it; training through failure teaches the failure pattern
- **Connective tissue load management** — ring work and wrist-loading skills place extraordinary demands on tendons that adapt 3–4× more slowly than muscles; see `connective_tissue` for the tissue-specific adaptation timeline and the gelatin + Vitamin C pre-training protocol

---

## Load Decision Framework

**When to advance a skill progression:**
Progress to the next step in a skill ladder only when 3 consecutive training sessions produce 3 × 5 reps (or 3 × 8–10 second holds for static skills) with ≥ 2 reps in reserve and no degradation in the final set. One exceptional session does not justify advancement — consistency across sessions is the standard.

**When to regress a skill progression:**
- Form breaks on the first or second rep of a set (not the last) → the current progression step is too advanced; drop one step and rebuild
- Wrist, elbow, or shoulder pain during or after training → immediately drop two progression steps and apply connective tissue protocol; do not train through joint pain in calisthenics
- Performance declines over two consecutive sessions at the same progression step → this is a fatigue accumulation signal, not a skill plateau; reduce total weekly volume before adjusting progression

**Weighted strength decision (pull-ups, dips):**
Standard double-progression: complete all prescribed sets at the top of the rep range with ≥ 2 RIR, then add 2.5 kg at the next session. For ring dips, add weight only when 3 × 10 bodyweight reps are achievable with full range and controlled tempo.

**The skill-to-strength ratio principle:**
At the intermediate level, the skill itself is the primary adaptation target. Strength work (weighted pull-ups, ring dips) exists to raise the ceiling of what skill work can achieve — not to replace it. If training time is limited, reduce strength volume before skill volume.

---

## Movement Category Definitions

### Vertical Pull (Muscle-Up Foundation)

- **Why this system requires this category:** The strict muscle-up requires pulling strength far above the pull-up ceiling — specifically, the ability to pull the chest to the bar rather than the chin. Weighted pull-ups at moderate rep ranges build the pulling capacity that makes the transition phase of the muscle-up mechanically possible.
- **What this means in this system:** Weighted pull-ups (primary), scapular pull-ups (mandatory activation), false grip dead hangs (muscle-up-specific preparation)
- **Selection criteria:** Full dead-hang starting position; no kipping during strength sets; strict pronated grip for carryover to muscle-up transition mechanics
- **The false grip requirement for ring muscle-ups:** The false grip (wrist over the ring rather than standard grip) must be established and trained before ring muscle-up attempts; attempting a ring muscle-up without false grip is attempting a different movement entirely
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 2 strength days + scapular activation every training day
- **Intensity range:** 4–6 RM range with added weight; scapular pull-ups: 3 × 12–15 bodyweight

---

### Ring Push (Muscle-Up and Planche Foundation)

- **Why this system requires this category:** Ring dips develop the pressing strength and shoulder stability required for the transition and support phase of the ring muscle-up, and establish the shoulder girdle control needed for early planche progressions.
- **What this means in this system:** Ring dips (primary), parallel bar dips (supplementary at higher load)
- **Selection criteria:** Rings turned out at the top of each rep (the "RTO" position); full depression at the bottom; no flared elbows
- **The rings-out (RTO) requirement:** Turning the rings out at lockout is not an optional style choice — it is the competition standard and the position that develops the rotational shoulder stability required for ring work. Training dips with rings in a neutral position builds a different strength pattern.
- **Why parallel bar dips supplement ring dips rather than replace them:** Parallel bars allow heavier loading; rings develop stability. Both are needed. Use parallel bars for load development when ring dips are limited by stability rather than strength.
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 2 days
- **Intensity range:** 5–8 RM; add weight when 3 × 10 bodyweight with full RTO is achievable

---

### Horizontal Push (One-Arm Progression Foundation)

- **Why this system requires this category:** Unilateral horizontal pushing strength builds the asymmetric force production required for planche progressions and eventual one-arm push-up variants, while maintaining the shoulder health balance against the high volume of pulling work.
- **What this means in this system:** Archer push-ups (primary, progressing from wide to narrow base), pseudo-planche push-ups (lean angle progression), standard push-ups with tempo control
- **Selection criteria:** Full range of motion (chest to floor); elbows at ≤ 45° flare; scapular protraction at the top
- **Progression logic:** Archer push-ups → one-arm negatives (5-second lowering) → assisted one-arm push-ups. Progress only when the weaker side controls 3 × 5 reps.
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 2 days
- **Intensity range:** 5–10 RM per side; tempo manipulation (3-second lowering) used before adding difficulty

---

### Horizontal Pull (Scapular Control and Row Strength)

- **Why this system requires this category:** Horizontal pulling balances the vertical pulling volume, develops rhomboid and mid-trap strength for scapular stability, and builds the back thickness required for high-load calisthenics progression.
- **What this means in this system:** Inverted rows (feet elevated or weighted vest), ring rows (for instability and increased range)
- **Selection criteria:** Full scapular retraction at the top of each rep; controlled lowering; body remains rigid (no hip sagging)
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 2 days
- **Intensity range:** 8–12 RM; elevate feet progressively to increase difficulty; add weight vest when bodyweight rows exceed 3 × 15 with control

---

### Core Tension (L-Sit and Hollow Body Foundation)

- **Why this system requires this category:** The hollow body position — posterior pelvic tilt, compressed ribcage, active shoulder depression — is the structural foundation of every calisthenics kipping movement (bar and ring muscle-up kip) and every static skill (L-sit, front lever, planche). It is not a "core exercise" in the conventional sense; it is the prerequisite motor pattern for all advanced calisthenics.
- **What this means in this system:** Hollow body holds and hollow body rocks (primary), tuck L-sit progression (parallettes preferred), Pallof press (anti-rotation support)
- **L-sit progression ladder:** Tuck L-sit (knees bent, both legs) → single-leg extension → full L-sit on parallettes → full L-sit on floor (much harder grip demand)
- **The hollow body standard:** At the intermediate level, the athlete must be able to hold a full hollow body (arms overhead, legs together, lower back pressed to floor) for 30 continuous seconds before advancing to ring or bar skill work that requires it. This is a prerequisite, not a warm-up.
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 3–4 days (included in every session; treated as activation rather than fatigue-inducing work)

---

### Overhead Pressing / Handstand Pathway (HSPU Foundation)

- **Why this system requires this category:** Wall HSPU is listed as an intermediate milestone but appears nowhere else in the movement category definitions — without this category, any training plan generated from this guide has no template for the pike push-up → HSPU progression. HSPU is an overhead strength skill on a completely different pressing plane from ring work; it must be developed along its own ladder.
- **What this means in this system:** Pike push-up (primary entry), elevated pike push-up (feet on box, progressively increasing body angle), wall HSPU with head to a pad, wall HSPU head to floor (full range)
- **Progression ladder:** Standard pike push-up → Elevated pike push-up (feet on 40 cm box → 60 cm box → hip height) → Wall HSPU with pad → Wall HSPU head to floor → Wall HSPU with deficit (hands on parallettes, head descends below hand level)
- **The overhead transfer caveat:** Ring dip strength does not transfer directly to HSPU — the pressing vector is vertical (overhead) rather than depression-dominant (dip), and the anterior deltoid and triceps recruitment pattern differ. An athlete with a strong ring dip may still fail an elevated pike push-up at high body angle because the movement demands are distinct. Train them separately.
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 2–3 days
- **Intensity range:** 5–8 RM at the current progression step; advance only when full range of motion is achieved with clean alignment across 3 sessions (no flared elbows, head touches pad or floor, full lockout at top)

---

### Unilateral Lower Body Pathway (Pistol Squat Foundation)

- **Why this system requires this category:** The pistol squat milestone appears in System Definition and Milestones but has no movement category to support it — no progression steps, no mobility prerequisites, no selection criteria. Without this category, pistol squat development is structurally orphaned from the training plan.
- **What this means in this system:** Assisted pistol squat (suspension trainer or door frame), box pistol (progressively lowering box height), counterbalance pistol, full pistol
- **Progression ladder:** Assisted pistol (suspension trainer at arm's length) → Box pistol (lowering to 45 cm box → 30 cm → parallel → below parallel) → Counterbalance pistol (arms extended forward) → Full pistol (arms free)
- **The three mobility prerequisites:** Full pistol simultaneously requires (1) ankle dorsiflexion (ankle-to-wall ≥ 10 cm, same-side foot flat), (2) hip flexion past parallel with the knee tracking over the foot, and (3) sufficient thoracic extension to prevent forward collapse. Any of these three can independently cap the progression — assess and address mobility limiters before treating the pistol as a pure strength problem.
- **The weaker side is the standard:** Progress is determined by the weaker leg. Never measure a milestone on the stronger side.
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 2 days
- **Intensity range:** 3–5 reps per side at the current step; advance when the weaker side completes the full rep count with consistent form across 3 consecutive sessions

---

## Milestones

### Technical Indicators

- 1 strict ring muscle-up: no kip, transition is smooth rather than a "chicken wing" (one elbow leading the other), full lockout at the top
- 5-second L-sit on parallettes with both legs fully extended and hips at or above handle height
- Wall handstand push-up: 5 reps with head touching the floor (not a pad) and full lockout at the top
- Pistol squat: 5 reps per side with full depth (hip crease below knee), heel flat, no lateral knee cave

### Strength / Fitness Indicators

- Weighted pull-up: +20 kg for 5 reps
- Ring dips: 3 × 10 bodyweight with full RTO at the top
- Hollow body hold: 40 continuous seconds
- Inverted row (feet elevated at bar height): 3 × 12

**Signal to advance to Advanced level:**
All four technical skill milestones achieved and the strength indicators met. Additionally, the athlete can demonstrate each skill for 3 consecutive reps with consistent form — single successful reps do not constitute a milestone.

---

## Common Violations

| Violation | Root Cause | Correct Approach |
|-----------|-----------|-----------------|
| Training skill sets after heavy strength work | Treating skill practice as just another exercise | Skill practice always precedes strength work; nervous system freshness is the training input for skills, not a luxury |
| Attempting ring muscle-ups without false grip | Attempting the movement before the grip pattern is established | False grip must be trained and comfortable under load (dead hang, rows) before muscle-up attempts begin |
| Skipping scapular pull-ups because they feel too easy | Confusing difficulty with effectiveness | Scapular pull-ups are injury prevention infrastructure; the shoulder stabilizers they develop take months to adapt and are irreplaceable |
| Adding weight to ring dips before rings-out is established | Prioritizing load over mechanics | Full RTO at lockout is a prerequisite for weighted ring dips, not something added later; loading a compromised position accelerates shoulder impingement |
| Continuing through wrist or elbow pain | Misclassifying tendon pain as muscle soreness | Tendon pain during calisthenics is a stop signal, not a push-through signal; see `connective_tissue` — tendon recovery operates on a 70–100 day timeline |
| Treating hollow body holds as optional | Viewing it as basic core work rather than foundational prerequisite | Hollow body is the structural prerequisite for every kipping movement and static skill; athletes who cannot hold a full hollow body for 30 seconds are building advanced skills on an unstable foundation |

# CrossFit Advanced / Competitor System Guide

## System Definition

**Goal:**
Peak physical performance across all ten CrossFit physical domains (strength, power, speed, endurance, stamina, flexibility, coordination, agility, balance, accuracy) for competitive Open preparation through a two-a-day training structure, systematic weakness targeting, and an 8–12 week Open-specific periodization cycle. The defining characteristic of the advanced CrossFit athlete is not training volume but movement economy under fatigue — highly efficient motor patterns that reduce the metabolic cost of every repetition, combined with an absolute strength reserve that makes WOD weights sub-maximal.

**Anti-goals:**
- Not maximizing training volume beyond physiological recovery limits — elite CrossFit athletes are not distinguished from advanced athletes by higher volume; they are distinguished by movement efficiency, pacing precision, and strength reserve; adding more hours beyond the recovery ceiling produces CNS burnout, not adaptation
- Not treating off-season and Open prep cycles identically — off-season prioritizes raw physiological adaptation (absolute strength, muscle hypertrophy, aerobic base, strict gymnastics); Open prep shifts to sport-specific fatigue tolerance, barbell cycling under metabolic distress, and pacing strategy
- Not programming every session at maximum intensity — the CNS has a finite daily recovery capacity; every near-maximal effort draws from this reserve; training at 100% effort daily keeps the autonomic nervous system in chronic sympathetic dominance, elevating cortisol and suppressing recovery
- Not structuring two-a-days with <4 hours between sessions — the AMPK signaling pathway activated by conditioning sessions requires 3 hours to return to baseline; strength training within this window directly inhibits the mTOR protein synthesis pathway; minimum 4–6 hours is required, 8 hours is optimal

---

## Weekly Structure Logic

| Work Type | Frequency | Session | Core Reason |
|-----------|-----------|---------|-------------|
| Monostructural aerobic development | 4–5 sessions/week | AM | AMPK pathway returns to baseline within 3 hours; AM aerobic work does not interfere with PM strength mTOR signaling |
| Strict gymnastics skill acquisition | 2–3 sessions/week | AM | Motor pattern development requires CNS-fresh state; AM before conditioning |
| Maximal strength or Olympic lifting (heavy) | 4–5 sessions/week | PM | mTOR response remains elevated for 18 hours post-resistance training; PM sessions allow full CNS recovery from AM work |
| High-intensity mixed-modal WOD | 4–5 sessions/week | PM | After strength component; WOD serves as the conditioning stimulus; the specific fatigue profile of a combined strength + WOD session mirrors competition conditions |
| Lactic sprint capacity | 1–2 sessions/week | PM | Short, maximal glycolytic efforts; requires 48 hours between sessions due to profound blood lactate elevation |
| Mandatory full rest | 1 day/week | — | Full physiological recovery; complete cessation of all training |
| Active recovery or mobility | 1–2 sessions/week | AM or standalone | Low-intensity movement — rowing at conversational pace, mobility work; does not draw meaningfully from CNS reserve |

**Energy system volume distribution (advanced/competitor):**

| System | Weekly Volume Target |
|--------|-------------------|
| Alactic (strength/power, <10 sec) | ~25% of total training volume |
| Lactic (glycolytic sprint, 10–120 sec) | ~35% of total training volume |
| Aerobic (oxidative, >120 sec) | ~40% of total training volume |

**Structural Constraints (hard rules):**

- **Minimum 4–6 hours between AM and PM sessions; 8+ hours is physiologically optimal** — the AMPK pathway from AM conditioning must return to baseline before PM strength work; < 4 hours produces direct molecular inhibition of mTOR protein synthesis and leaves glycogen partially depleted for the strength session
- **CNS-intensive work (heavy Olympic lifting, maximal strength, high-skill gymnastics) is always PM** — the AM session must remain at low CNS demand; AM high-intensity work eliminates the capacity for quality PM work
- **All structured training ceases 2 days before competition** — muscle glycogen stores need 48 hours to supercompensate after the final loading session; training within 2 days of competition reduces fast-twitch fiber excitability and glycogen stores on meet day
- **Open prep taper is 5–7 days, not 2–3 weeks** — CrossFit taper is shorter than powerlifting taper because the sport demands skill execution under fatigue, not pure 1RM expression; a 2-week CrossFit taper causes work capacity detraining
- **One weakness is targeted at a time** — attempting to fix multiple weaknesses simultaneously competes for recovery resources and dilutes the training stimulus for each; identify the single greatest limiter and bias the program toward it for 4–8 weeks

---

## Load Decision Framework

**Two-a-day session quality monitoring:**
Each PM session must be evaluated against baseline performance. If PM session quality is noticeably degraded (slower bar speed, decreased gymnastic capacity, higher perceived effort at standard loads) on more than 2 consecutive days, the AM workload is too high — reduce AM session intensity or duration until PM quality is restored.

**Open prep periodization (8–12 weeks out):**
- **8–12 weeks out:** Maintain high-volume GPP; emphasize weakness domain with bias block; 60–70% maintenance volume on non-priority domains
- **5–8 weeks out:** Introduce Open-style WODs (high-repetition fatigue tolerance, mixed modal, uncertain time domain); shift ratio toward sport-specific mixed-modal conditioning
- **2–5 weeks out:** Peak sport-specific intensity; WODs resemble Open workout structure; reduce pure strength volume to maintenance; sharpen barbell cycling efficiency
- **1–2 weeks out:** Taper — reduce volume 41% while maintaining intensity and frequency; no new stimuli; practice pacing strategies on known WODs
- **2 days out:** Complete rest; allow glycogen supercompensation

**Weakness targeting decision framework:**
Diagnose weakness through benchmark testing:
- Strength deficit: 1RM back squat <2.0× BW or deadlift <2.0× BW → prioritize strength bias block
- Aerobic engine deficit: 2km row >7:00 (male) → prioritize Zone 2 aerobic volume block
- Lactic power deficit: 500m row >1:35 or max 60-second Assault bike output below regional standard → lactic sprint block
- Technical capacity deficit: maximum unbroken ring muscle-ups <5 → gymnastics skill block

**Bias block structure:** Maintain GPP at 60–70% of normal volume; allocate 30–40% of training time to targeted weakness work programmed at the start of sessions when CNS is fresh.

---

## Movement Category Definitions

### Alactic Strength Development

- **Why this system requires this category at the advanced level:** The strength reserve concept scales proportionally — a back squat of 2.5× BW makes competition thrusters represent 30–35% of 1RM; the same weight for an athlete squatting 1.5× BW represents 50% of 1RM. This ratio determines exactly how much energy every thruster costs for the entire WOD.
- **What this typically means in this system:** Heavy barbell work using conjugate or block periodization (not simple linear or wave models); max-effort squat and press variations rotating every 1–3 weeks; Olympic lifting singles at 85–90%
- **Advanced strength thresholds:** At advanced level, target back squat ≥2.0× BW, deadlift ≥2.5× BW, 1RM clean & jerk ≥1.25–1.35× BW, snatch ≥1.00–1.15× BW
- **Minimum PM frequency:** 4–5 sessions/week heavy barbell work

---

### Lactic Glycolytic Power

- **Why this system requires this category:** Open workouts frequently follow the 2–8 minute time domain of classic benchmark WODs (Fran-format); without dedicated glycolytic training, power output in these time domains cannot be developed from mixed-modal AMRAPs alone.
- **What this typically means in this system:** Sprint WOD repeats (modified Fran at target time ±30 seconds), 500m row repeats, Assault bike 60-second maximal efforts, Isabel-format barbell cycling
- **Recovery requirement:** 48 hours between maximal lactic sessions; blood lactate reaches 13–18 mmol/L during peak-intensity CrossFit efforts; incomplete recovery before the next lactic session produces cumulative performance decline
- **Advanced standard:** Fran in 3:00–4:30; Grace in 2:15–3:00; ability to complete Fran unbroken (all 45 thrusters and 45 pull-ups without breaking sets)

---

### Aerobic Engine (Oxidative Base)

- **Why this system requires this category:** At the advanced level, the aerobic base serves primarily as the recovery rate between WOD rounds and the platform for all other energy systems — a stronger aerobic base means faster PCr resynthesis, slower power decline between rounds, and more consistent transitions.
- **What this typically means in this system:** Zone 2 rowing, running, or cycling at conversational pace (60–70% max heart rate); Cindy-type AMRAPs; longer monostructural aerobic repeats (4×1km row at 80–85% effort)
- **Distribution at advanced level:** Aerobic work drops to ~40% of total volume (vs. 60% at intermediate) as lactic and alactic capacity become the primary performance differentiators at this level
- **Advanced aerobic standard:** Cindy 20–23 rounds (male); Murph in 35–40 minutes with vest

---

### Gymnastics Skill Execution (Dynamic and Cyclic)

- **Why this system requires this category:** Advanced CrossFit athletes must execute gymnastics movements not just correctly but efficiently under metabolic distress — butterfly pull-ups maintained at 85%+ of max-unbroken count when heart rate is at 90% max.
- **What this typically means in this system:** Butterfly pull-up capacity development (primary pulling modality for fast-paced WODs), bar and ring muscle-up cycling, kipping HSPU efficiency (high-rep tolerance), handstand walk (Open-specific skill), toes-to-bar unbroken at 20+ reps
- **Advanced skill standards:**

| Skill | Advanced Male Standard |
|-------|----------------------|
| Kipping pull-ups | Maintained sets of 20+ under WOD conditions |
| Butterfly pull-ups | Primary pull-up modality in fast WODs |
| Bar muscle-up | 5+ unbroken in fatigue; used in high-volume metcons |
| Ring muscle-up | 5+ unbroken; practiced at 20% of max unbroken capacity under fatigue |
| Kipping HSPU | High-rep touch-and-go; default vertical pressing standard |
| Handstand walk | Long-distance segments + obstacle navigation under metabolic distress |

- **Programming placement:** Strict gymnastics skill practice in AM sessions; high-rep cyclic gymnastics in PM WOD context

---

### Olympic Lifting Under Fatigue (Barbell Cycling)

- **Why this system requires this category:** Open workouts like Isabel (30 snatches) and Grace (30 clean and jerks) demand barbell cycling proficiency — a distinct motor skill from single-rep competition weightlifting.
- **What this typically means in this system:** High-rep barbell cycling at 50–60% 1RM with continuous touch-and-go mechanics; technique adaptation for competition CrossFit context: more horizontal bar path, wider foot placement, power receiving rather than full squat receiving
- **Advanced standard under fatigue:** Isabel under 2:30 (male); clean & jerk cycling at competition weights maintains consistent bar path across all 30 reps
- **Critical distinction:** DO NOT apply competitive weightlifting technique cues (maximize bar height, catch in full squat, minimize horizontal bar displacement) during barbell cycling — the CrossFit cycling pattern prioritizes turnover speed and sustainable rhythm over positional perfection

## Common Violations

|-----------|-----------|-----------------|
| Two-a-days with <4 hours between sessions | Scheduling convenience or ambition exceeding recovery capacity | AMPK from AM conditioning requires 3 hours to return to baseline before PM mTOR strength signaling can operate uninhibited; <4-hour gaps produce direct molecular interference with hypertrophy and strength adaptation |

| Tapering 2–3 weeks before the Open (powerlifting-style taper) | Applying periodization from other strength sports | CrossFit performance requires work capacity and fatigue tolerance, not just 1RM expression; a 2-week taper causes detraining of the aerobic and metabolic systems that CrossFit benchmarks depend on; 5–7 days is the correct CrossFit taper |

# CrossFit Intermediate System Guide

## System Definition

**Goal:**
Develop the capacity to perform most daily WODs at the prescribed Rx standard by building a broad aerobic base and sufficient barbell strength reserve, while acquiring the foundational gymnastics skill set (kipping pull-up, toes-to-bar, double-unders, strict HSPU, beginning kipping HSPU). The central principle at the intermediate level is that the aerobic engine and basic strength reserve determine the ceiling for all other CrossFit capacities — attempting to develop advanced gymnastics skills or high-skill Olympic lifting before these foundations are established produces premature performance plateaus.

**Anti-goals:**
- Not attempting Rx weights when they exceed 60–65% of 1RM for WODs designed as glycolytic sprints — using too-heavy loads in a 3-minute workout transforms it into a 12-minute grind, losing the intended metabolic stimulus and accumulating CNS fatigue without developing glycolytic capacity
- Not neglecting the aerobic base in favor of exclusively high-intensity WODs — lactate clearance, phosphocreatine resynthesis, and intrasession recovery between rounds are all oxidative processes; an underdeveloped aerobic base causes steep power decline across WOD rounds
- Not attempting kipping or butterfly movements before establishing strict capacity — kipping pull-ups expose the glenohumeral joint to distraction forces that the passive structures (labrum, capsule) can only absorb safely when the rotator cuff provides active stabilization, which requires established strict pull-up strength
- Not overreaching with two-a-day training — intermediate athletes do not yet have the recovery infrastructure to absorb two daily sessions; adding volume beyond 4–5 sessions/week is counterproductive before capacity foundations are set

---

## Weekly Structure Logic

| Work Type | Weekly Frequency | Core Reason |
|-----------|-----------------|-------------|
| Squat or posterior chain strength | 1–2 days | Building a back squat of 2.0× BW provides the strength reserve that makes barbell metcon movements (thrusters, wall balls, kettlebell swings) sub-maximal; below this threshold, every repetition is near-maximal and metabolically expensive |
| Barbell pressing and pulling (overhead strength) | 1–2 days | Overhead work directly supports kipping HSPU development and barbell cycling in overhead WODs; strict HSPU cannot be developed from pressing metcons alone |
| Olympic lifting technical practice | 1 day | Snatch and clean & jerk mechanics must be ingrained to automated motor patterns before they can be executed under metabolic fatigue; dedicated technical days develop this without the degradation of fatigued practice |
| Short lactic WOD (2–8 minutes) | 1–2 days | Develops glycolytic power and lactate tolerance required for benchmark sprint WODs (Fran, Grace, Isabel); must be trained at the correct scale to stay in the intended time domain |
| Moderate aerobic WOD (10–20 minutes) | 1–2 days | Builds oxidative base; most benchmark WODs at intermediate level fall in this time domain |
| Strict gymnastics skill practice | 2–3 days (within sessions, before WOD) | Strict capacity (pull-ups, dips, HSPU) must be trained before kipping; 10–15 minutes of strict work at the start of sessions before fatigue accumulates |
| Mandatory full rest | 2 days | Thursday and one weekend day; incomplete recovery at intermediate level is the primary cause of chronic performance plateau |

**Structural Constraints (hard rules):**

- **Strength and skill work precedes the WOD in every shared session** — the WOD produces profound metabolic fatigue that degrades technical quality; performing a strength or skill component after the WOD means training the wrong motor pattern under fatigue; the correct order is always: skill/strength → WOD
- **Olympic lifting is never programmed under glycolytic fatigue without first establishing automated technique** — under lactic acidosis, bar path horizontal displacement increases, range of motion consistency decreases, and structural compensation (lumbar flexion, knee valgus) appears; athletes who have not deeply ingrained the movement patterns will default to injury-risk positions
- **Strict pull-up capacity must precede kipping work** — 3–5 strict pull-ups (male) / 2 strict pull-ups (female) is the minimum before introducing kipping; without this, the glenohumeral joint's passive structures absorb the ballistic distraction forces at the bottom of the kip, leading to labral tears and rotator cuff pathology
- **Scale WOD load to keep execution within the intended time domain** — benchmark sprint WODs (Fran, Grace, Diane) are designed to take 2–8 minutes; if Rx weights extend execution to 15+ minutes, the glycolytic training effect is lost and CNS fatigue accumulates without metabolic adaptation

---

## Load Decision Framework

**WOD scaling decision:**
Scale load to a percentage of 1RM that keeps the WOD within the intended time domain:
- Glycolytic sprint WODs (Fran, Grace, Isabel): barbell load ≤50–55% of 1RM ensures continuous bar cycling
- Aerobic threshold WODs (Helen, Cindy): load at a level that allows unbroken sets or minimal rest in the first round
- If unsure: the intermediate standard for a WOD time serves as the primary filter; if the Rx load would put you significantly above the intermediate time standard, scale down

**Strength progression:**
- Main strength exercises (squat, press, deadlift): apply double-progression (work a rep range, increase load when upper range achieved at ≥2 RIR)
- Do not chase 1RM testing during the training cycle — reserve maximal efforts for end-of-block testing; frequent 1RM attempts accumulate CNS fatigue without producing strength gains

**Gymnastics progression:**
- Progress a gymnastics skill to the next step only when 3 consecutive sessions demonstrate the current step with controlled technique and no degradation
- Never train kipping skills to technical failure — form breakdown teaches the failure pattern; stop before technique degrades

**When to deload:**
Every 5–6 weeks, or when performance in WODs declines for two consecutive sessions at the same scale, reduce total volume by 40–50% for one week while maintaining training frequency and intensity. CrossFit trainees frequently underestimate accumulated metabolic fatigue; a deload week typically produces notable performance improvements in the first session back.

---

## Movement Category Definitions

### Alactic Strength and Power (0–10 Seconds)

- **Why this system requires this category:** The strength reserve concept — the ratio of WOD load to 1RM — directly determines how much energy each barbell repetition costs. Every kilogram added to a 1RM front squat reduces the metabolic cost of every thruster repetition in every future WOD.
- **What this typically means in this system:** Back squat, deadlift, strict press, power clean (at heavy loads, low reps, full recovery between sets)
- **Training structure:** EMOM or interval sets with 1:10+ work:rest ratio; 1–3 reps per set; complete phosphocreatine resynthesis between efforts (2–5 minutes)
- **Strength reserve thresholds:** A back squat of 2.0× BW and deadlift of 2.0–2.5× BW are the upper bounds of practical utility for CrossFit metcons — beyond these, the returns on further absolute strength development are logarithmically diminishing relative to the cost
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 1–2 dedicated strength days before WOD work

---

### Lactic Glycolytic Conditioning (10–120 Seconds)

- **Why this system requires this category:** The benchmark sprint WODs (Fran, Grace, Isabel, Diane) are designed as pure lactic efforts. Developing glycolytic power and lactate tolerance requires training the correct time domain — if every WOD is scaled to a 15-minute grind, glycolytic capacity never develops.
- **What this typically means in this system:** Fran-format WODs (short rep schemes, high intensity), 500m row sprints, Assault bike intervals, 3-round sprint couplets
- **Training structure:** Maximal non-stop efforts lasting 45–90 seconds, or WODs targeting 2–8-minute completion time; full 3–5 minute rest between repeat efforts to prevent accumulation of glycolytic fatigue across sets
- **Blood lactate context:** High-intensity CrossFit WODs produce blood lactate of 13–18 mmol/L; full metabolic recovery requires up to 48 hours; lactic sessions must not appear on consecutive days
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 1–2 days/week

---

### Aerobic Oxidative Conditioning (>120 Seconds)

- **Why this system requires this category:** The aerobic system is the primary mechanism for clearing lactate between rounds, resynthesizing phosphocreatine during rest intervals, and sustaining consistent output across multi-round WODs. Athletes with a weak aerobic base show steep power decline across rounds; athletes with a strong aerobic base maintain consistent splits.
- **What this typically means in this system:** Helen, Cindy, rowing intervals, running, long mixed-modal AMRAPs (15–25 minutes)
- **Zone 2 training:** Occasional low-intensity monostructural sessions (easy rowing, running, or cycling) at conversational pace develop mitochondrial density and capillary density without adding to cumulative CNS and metabolic load
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 1–2 days/week

---

### Gymnastics Skill Development (Strict Foundation)

- **Why this system requires this category:** Gymnastics movements in CrossFit — pull-ups, muscle-ups, HSPUs, toes-to-bar — appear in the majority of benchmark WODs. Unlike barbell movements, gymnastics capacity cannot be scaled purely by load — the movement itself requires strict strength before dynamic (kipping) variations are introduced.
- **What this typically means in this system:** Strict pull-ups, ring rows, hollow body holds, kipping pull-up technique (after strict standard is met), toes-to-bar, wall handstands, pike push-ups → strict HSPU progression
- **Progression rules:**
  - Kipping pull-up requires ≥3–5 strict pull-ups (male) / ≥2 strict pull-ups (female)
  - Kipping HSPU requires ≥5 strict HSPUs + tripod stability
  - Bar muscle-up requires ≥10–20 chest-to-bar pull-ups
- **Programming placement:** Always in the first 10–15 minutes of the session, before any metabolic conditioning; strict gymnastics under fatigue produces incorrect motor patterns
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** Gymnastics skill work appears in 2–3 sessions/week

---

### Olympic Lifting Technical Development

- **Why this system requires this category:** The snatch and clean & jerk appear in multiple benchmark WODs and Open workouts. CrossFit barbell cycling (high-rep, touch-and-go) requires a distinct motor pattern from competitive weightlifting — a more horizontal bar path, wider foot placement, and reliance on power receiving rather than full squat.
- **What this typically means in this system:** Power clean, power snatch, hang clean (less eccentric demand), barbell cycling at moderate loads (50–60% 1RM for conditioning; 80%+ for strength technical work)
- **Technical floor:** Safe positional execution of the front squat, overhead squat, and power snatch at moderate loads before introducing Olympic lifting into fatigued conditioning
- **Skill benchmarks for intermediate:** Snatch 0.50–0.75× BW; Clean & Jerk 0.75–1.00× BW
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 1 dedicated technical session/week

- ≥5 unbroken strict pull-ups (male) / ≥2 unbroken strict pull-ups (female)
- Consistent kipping mechanics: hollow-to-arch position without spinal collapse on the bar
- Unbroken double-unders (≥20)
- Safe positional execution of power clean, power snatch, and front squat under moderate load
- Beginning kipping HSPU (with abmat scaling or box substitute) if strict HSPU is not yet achieved

| Violation | Root Cause | Correct Approach |
|-----------|-----------|-----------------|
| Performing Rx weights that extend WOD duration beyond intended time domain ("Rx Ego Trap") | Confusing Rx completion with appropriate scaling | A 3-minute glycolytic WOD done in 15 minutes at Rx has a completely different metabolic profile than the intended stimulus; scale load to keep execution within the target time domain |

# Olympic Weightlifting Advanced System Guide

## System Definition

**Goal:**
Maximize snatch and clean & jerk performance through daily exposure to competition lifts at near-maximal intensity, within a 16-week block structure that peaks precisely on a designated competition date. At the advanced level, the lift itself is both the training tool and the performance target — there is no separation between "preparing for the lift" and "doing the lift." Every session is, in effect, a rehearsal of competition.

**Anti-goals:**
- Not developing metabolic conditioning — rest periods are determined by technical readiness, not time; 5–10 minute rests between maximal attempts are appropriate and expected
- Not pursuing hypertrophy — bodyweight is managed within a weight class; adding mass that does not directly serve force production in the two lifts is a competitive liability
- Not replicating powerlifting training logic — the goal is not a heavier squat or pull as an end in itself; these exist only insofar as they enable heavier snatches and cleans
- Not introducing technical experiments near competition — movement patterns that have taken years to establish cannot be meaningfully changed in a peaking block; changes introduced under maximal load in the final weeks create inconsistency, not improvement

---

## Weekly Structure Logic

| Work Type | Weekly Frequency | Core Reason |
|-----------|-----------------|-------------|
| Snatch (full competition lift, daily max) | 5–6 days | Frequency is the irreducible driver of maximal lift performance; no other training stimulus substitutes for lift exposure |
| Clean & Jerk (full competition lift, daily max) | 5–6 days | Same as snatch; the two lifts share approximately equal session time |
| Front squat | 2–3 days | Direct receiving strength; higher priority than back squat at all times |
| Back squat | 2–3 days | General leg strength base; total squat days (front + back combined) capped at 4 days/week |
| Snatch pull and Clean pull | 1–2 days | Targeted neural overload only — high-frequency pulls are physiologically incompatible with 5–6 day daily max training; combining both overloads the lumbar spine and patellar tendons beyond recoverable limits |
| Overhead squat | 2 days | Positional maintenance for snatch receiving; diagnostic function |
| Power versions (power snatch, power clean) | 3 days | Used as warm-up and technical diagnostic — not as independent training emphasis |

**Structural Constraints (hard rules):**

- **Daily max sessions require full neural readiness before beginning working sets** — if the athlete is not able to produce a clean lift at 80% within the first 3–4 warm-up sets, the session load should be reduced; pushing for daily maxes when the nervous system is not ready produces degraded movement patterns under maximal load
- **Back-off sets are mandatory every session** — after the daily max is established, 2–3 back-off singles at 80–85% of the session maximum must be completed; skipping back-off sets to protect the feeling of a successful heavy single is a structural error (see below)
- **Power versions serve as diagnostic tools, not as competing training emphasis** — if the power snatch at 75% shows a different bar path than 6 weeks ago, the lift mechanics have shifted; this is actionable information, not a training goal in itself
- **Deload weeks are non-negotiable** — within a 16-week cycle, weeks 8 and 14 are deload weeks; the daily max cap is set at 85% and no personal records are attempted; the nervous system cannot sustain maximal daily effort for 16 consecutive weeks

---

## Load Decision Framework

**Daily max approach:**
Work up to the heaviest single that can be lifted with competition-standard technique. This is not a fixed percentage — it is the heaviest weight available on that specific day, in that specific neuromuscular state. On a good day, this may be 97–100% of all-time best. On a neutral day, 88–92%. On a poor day, 82–86%. The goal is to find the session ceiling, not to match a number from last week.

**When to stop climbing:**
- Form degrades visibly in the receiving position or during the pull
- Bar speed in the first and second pull decreases noticeably compared to earlier warm-up sets
- The previous attempt required significant grinding to complete
Stop at that weight. Attempts after these signals produce injury-reinforcing movement, not adaptation.

**The back-off set principle:**
Back-off sets at 80–85% of the daily max are the primary volume accumulation mechanism in daily max training. They matter more than the daily max single itself. An athlete who consistently hits 85% and completes all back-off sets will outperform an athlete who occasionally hits 97% and skips back-off sets. Volume at high intensity is the adaptation driver; the daily max single is a performance indicator, not the training stimulus.

**Competition cycle load management:**
- Weeks 1–5 (Accumulation): daily maxes at approximately 88–92% of all-time best; volume is higher
- Weeks 6–10 (Intensification): push daily maxes toward and occasionally past all-time best; volume decreases
- Weeks 11–14 (Peaking): daily maxes at competition-realistic weights; attempt selection practice begins
- Weeks 15–16 (Taper): volume −50%; lift only at opener and second-attempt weights; no maximal effort

---

## Movement Category Definitions

### Full Competition Snatch

- **What this system requires at the advanced level:** Every snatch session begins with building to a daily max, followed by back-off sets. The snatch is not periodized in the traditional sense — it is practiced at high intensity daily, with the daily max varying naturally based on recovery and neuromuscular readiness.
- **Bar path standard:** At the advanced level, the bar path must be internally consistent — the athlete's bar path at 90% should not differ meaningfully from their bar path at 70%. A bar path that changes under load indicates that the technique is not yet automatic; this is an intermediate-level problem that must be resolved before the advanced approach is appropriate.
- **The snatch-to-back-squat ratio as a diagnostic:** A snatch below 70% of back squat 1RM indicates the snatch is being limited by technique, not strength. A snatch above 80% of back squat 1RM indicates the strength base may be the limiting factor. Monitor this ratio over the training cycle.
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 5–6 days
- **Session structure:** 3–4 technical warm-up sets → progressive loading to daily max → 2–3 back-off singles at 80–85% of daily max

---

### Full Competition Clean & Jerk

- **What this system requires at the advanced level:** The clean and jerk may be practiced as a complex (clean, then jerk) or separated (clean to front squat; jerk from rack) depending on what the session is targeting. Separated practice is used when one component needs specific attention; competition-standard complex practice is used in peaking blocks.
- **The clean-to-jerk transition:** At the advanced level, the pause between clean and jerk is a deliberate component of the lift, not a rest. The athlete uses this pause to establish foot position, control breathing, and set the dip start position. Athletes who rush the jerk immediately after the clean without this reset are practicing competition anxiety management, not technique.
- **Rack reset before the jerk (advanced micro-technique):** At heavy loads, many failed jerks are not caused by insufficient pressing strength but by a compromised rack position after the clean — the bar slides off the deltoid shelf, compresses the airway, or shifts the grip. Before initiating the dip, the athlete must execute a deliberate rack reset: slightly lower the elbows to re-seat the bar on the anterior deltoid shelf, widen the grip fractionally if needed to release wrist tension, and take a full controlled breath. This reset takes 1–2 seconds and is not optional under heavy load. Athletes who skip the reset and dip immediately out of an unstable rack are initiating the jerk from a mechanically compromised position.
- **Jerk-specific constraint at the advanced level:** The split jerk landing position must be mechanically locked — front foot flat, back foot on the ball, hips under the bar, both elbows locked simultaneously. A jerk that requires a press-out to complete is a failed attempt by competition standard; training pressed-out jerks trains the wrong completion pattern.
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 5–6 days
- **Session structure:** Same as snatch — warm-up sets → daily max → back-off sets

---

### Front Squat

- **What this system requires at the advanced level:** The front squat must be loaded to levels that challenge the clean receiving position under heavier-than-competition loads. If the front squat is not meaningfully above the clean 1RM, the athlete will encounter a strength ceiling in the clean before a technical ceiling.
- **Loading target:** Front squat should reach 130–140% of clean 1RM as a long-term goal. In a 16-week cycle, program front squat to peak in weeks 10–12 (before the peaking block reduces volume).
- **The back squat / front squat balance:** Back squat is trained alongside front squat but always at a lower priority. The common error is increasing back squat load at the expense of front squat frequency. Monitor the ratio: back squat should not exceed 130% of front squat 1RM (if it does, front squat is underreached).
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 2–3 days
- **Intensity range:** 80–93% of front squat 1RM; sets of 2–3

---

### Pulls (Snatch Pull and Clean Pull)

- **What this system requires at the advanced level:** Pulls are the primary overload mechanism for the first and second pull phases without the neural cost of a full lift. At the advanced level, pull loads significantly exceed lift loads — snatch pull at 105–115% of snatch 1RM; clean pull at 105–115% of clean 1RM.
- **Pulls as fatigue management tools:** When the athlete is heavily fatigued but the program calls for pulling strength stimulus, pulls can replace full lifts without the same technical degradation risk. Full lifts under heavy fatigue train failure patterns; pulls under heavy fatigue train strength.
- **Pull technique must mirror lift technique exactly:** The bar path, foot pressure distribution, and hip extension timing in a pull must be identical to the corresponding lift. A pull that differs from the lift in any of these parameters is training a different motor pattern, which will compete with — rather than support — the competition lift.
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 1–2 days
- **Intensity range:** 105–115% of corresponding lift 1RM; sets of 3

---

### Power Versions (Power Snatch and Power Clean)

- **Their role at the advanced level:** Power versions are used exclusively as warm-up tools and technical diagnostics — they are not an independent training emphasis. This is a categorical departure from intermediate programming, where power versions were used as learning tools.
- **As a diagnostic:** Performing 2–3 power snatch reps at 70–75% of snatch 1RM at the start of a snatch session reveals whether the pull mechanics are functioning correctly that day. If the power snatch looks different from the previous session, something in the pull has shifted and the session load should be adjusted accordingly.
- **Why they are not trained as separate events:** At the advanced level, every technical repetition should reinforce the competition lift pattern. Power versions do not include the catch position that must be maintained in competition; using them as primary training means the athlete is not practicing the most specific version of the movement.
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 3 days (as warm-up only; not programmed as separate working sets)

---

## Milestones

### Technical Indicators

- Snatch bar path is visually identical at 80% and 95% of 1RM — no trajectory shift under load
- Clean receiving position (elbows parallel or above) is maintained on every rep without cueing, including the final rep of a back-off set when fatigued
- Jerk receiving position is mechanically locked within 0.5 seconds of foot strike — no press-out is required on any training attempt
- Power snatch percentage of squat snatch 1RM is ≥ 87% — indicating the catch position is adding load, not saving failed pulls

### Strength / Fitness Indicators

- Snatch ≥ 1.25–1.4× bodyweight (male), ≥ 1.0–1.1× bodyweight (female)
- Clean & Jerk ≥ 1.5–1.7× bodyweight (male), ≥ 1.25–1.4× bodyweight (female)
- Front Squat ≥ 150% of bodyweight
- Back Squat ≥ 180% of bodyweight

**Routing warning:** Values below 1.25× BW snatch (male) or 1.0× BW snatch (female) indicate intermediate-level performance. Athletes at these numbers should not be routed to 5–6 day daily max programming — the joint and connective tissue adaptation required for that training density has not been established.

**Signal of reaching the top of the advanced tier:**
Competition results are within 3–5% of training maxima consistently across multiple competitions — indicating that the peaking process is reliable, not that training is still the bottleneck.

---

## Common Violations

| Violation | Root Cause | Correct Approach |
|-----------|-----------|-----------------|
| Skipping back-off sets after achieving a daily max | Protecting the feeling of a successful heavy lift | Back-off sets are the primary training stimulus; the daily max single is a performance indicator. Never skip back-off sets. |
| Forcing a daily max when the warm-up sets show poor form | Commitment to a number rather than a session | Reduce the session ceiling when early warm-up sets diverge from established mechanics; a 90% session with clean mechanics outperforms a failed 98% attempt |
| Replacing front squat with back squat as competition approaches | Perceived higher strength output from back squat | Front squat frequency must be maintained through the peaking block; the receiving position is what is being competed, not the squat |
| Introducing technical changes in the peaking block | Identifying a flaw during heavy training and attempting to correct it under competition conditions | Technical changes require at least 8 weeks of deliberate practice to become reliable under load; note the issue and address it in the next training cycle |
| Training through form breakdown because a meet is near | Urgency overriding long-term motor pattern integrity | Poor technique under maximal load trains a worse movement pattern than stopping early; a missed session in the final weeks is less costly than 6 weeks of reinforced incorrect mechanics |
| Neglecting the deload weeks in a 16-week cycle | Feeling strong and not wanting to reduce load | Deload weeks are not optional recovery — they are planned supercompensation windows; skipping them shifts the peak timing and often results in suboptimal competition performance |

# Olympic Weightlifting Intermediate System Guide

## System Definition

**Goal:**
Transition from power versions of the competition lifts to full squat versions with consistent receiving positions, while simultaneously developing the strength base (front squat, pulls) required to support heavier loads in the catch. The defining task at the intermediate level is not lifting heavier weight — it is learning to receive the bar correctly at depth, and training the body to be strong in those positions.

**Anti-goals:**
- Not maximizing absolute strength — front squat and pull strength exist to serve the competition lifts, not as ends in themselves; a lifter who can front squat 200 kg but cannot receive a 120 kg clean is not more advanced
- Not developing aerobic conditioning — Olympic lifting sessions are entirely rest-based; any cardiovascular fatigue carried into a working set degrades technique
- Not pursuing hypertrophy — muscle mass that does not contribute to force production in the specific positions of the snatch and clean & jerk is non-functional weight in a weight-class sport
- Not chasing daily maxima before positions are established — an athlete who attempts personal records before the catch positions are reliable is training failure patterns, not success patterns

---

## Weekly Structure Logic

| Work Type | Weekly Frequency | Core Reason |
|-----------|-----------------|-------------|
| Snatch (power or squat depending on phase) | 3–4 days | Frequency is the primary driver of technical improvement in Olympic lifting |
| Clean & Jerk (power or squat depending on phase) | 3–4 days | Same as snatch; the two lifts should receive roughly equal exposure |
| Front squat | 2–3 days | The most sport-specific strength exercise; determines receiving strength in the clean |
| Overhead squat | 2 days | Maintains snatch receiving position integrity; a failing overhead squat predicts a failing snatch |
| Pulls (snatch pull, clean pull) | 3 days | Builds pull mechanics through volume at controlled intensity; not a neural overload tool at this level |
| Back squat | 1–2 days | Secondary strength builder; lower priority than front squat at this level |

**Structural Constraints (hard rules):**

- **Snatch and clean & jerk are not both trained at near-maximal intensity on the same day** — technical quality on the second lift is always compromised; schedule one as heavy work and one as technical/moderate volume, or separate them across days
- **Front squat is scheduled 2–3 days per week** — this is the single most important accessory lift in Olympic weightlifting; athletes who deprioritize it in favor of back squat systematically develop a weak receiving position
- **Pulls are periodized across training phases** — Accumulation: snatch pull 95–105% of snatch 1RM, clean pull 95–105% of clean 1RM (bar path accuracy priority); Intensification: 110–120% of the corresponding lift 1RM (peak force overload); Peaking: return to 95–100%. The pull must overload the extension phase; if the pull equals the lift weight, no overload stimulus is occurring
- **Overhead squat is maintained every week without exception** — the overhead squat is a positional diagnostic tool as much as a strength builder; a drop in overhead squat quality almost always precedes a technical breakdown in the snatch by 1–2 weeks

---

## Load Decision Framework

**When to increase load:**
Increase weight on the competition lifts only when the current load is being received in a consistent position across multiple reps and sessions — not when a single successful lift occurred. The standard is: the last 3 sessions at this weight all produced technically sound lifts at a similar RPE. For squats and pulls: standard double-progression (complete top of rep range with RIR ≥ 2, add load).

**When to reduce load:**
- More than 2 missed attempts at a given weight in a single session → reduce to a weight that can be made cleanly and end there; grinding through misses reinforces incorrect movement patterns
- The power version (power snatch or power clean) begins to deteriorate at a load previously handled easily → the pull is degrading; address pull mechanics before increasing squat catch weight
- The receiving position is visibly changing under load (forward lean in the clean receiving position, elbows dropping in the front rack, early arm pull in the snatch) → reduce weight until the position is recovered

**The power version as a diagnostic tool:**
The power snatch and power clean serve two purposes at the intermediate level: warm-up exposure and technique diagnosis. If the power snatch at 70% of 1RM looks different from three weeks ago, something in the pull mechanics has changed. Use power versions intentionally as checkpoints, not only as transitional exercises.

**Session structure principle:**
Every session begins with technique work at submaximal load. Attempting to work up to a heavy single without establishing position at 60–70% first is a structural error. The heavy work exists to challenge the positions already established in the warm-up sets.

---

## Movement Category Definitions

### Snatch Pathway

- **Why this system requires this category:** The snatch is the first competition lift and the technically more demanding of the two. All snatch-direction training serves to improve the athlete's ability to receive the bar overhead at depth with elbows locked and the bar over the mid-foot.
- **Progression ladder within the intermediate phase:** Power snatch (no depth requirement) → Hang snatch above knee (teaches the finish position) → Hang snatch below knee (adds the transition) → Squat snatch from the floor (full movement). Movement through this ladder is position-dependent, not time-dependent.
- **Selection criteria for session emphasis:** Use hang variations when the problem is in the second pull or turnover; use floor snatches when building competition-standard movement; use power snatch as warm-up and diagnostic
- **Technical bottleneck most common at this level:** The early arm pull — athletes begin pulling with the arms before the hips have completed extension, reducing bar height and pulling the bar away from the body. This is a pull timing error, corrected by snatch pulls and high-pull drills, not by heavier snatches.
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 3 days
- **Intensity range:** Technical work: 65–80% 1RM; heavy singles: 85–95% when positions are reliable

---

### Clean & Jerk Pathway

- **Why this system requires this category:** The clean & jerk is the second competition lift and allows heavier absolute loads than the snatch. The jerk is a distinct sub-skill from the clean and requires dedicated practice separate from clean work.
- **The clean and the jerk are separated in training:** Clean practice can occur without jerk (power clean + front squat), and jerk practice can occur without clean (jerk from rack). Combining them in every session creates a session structure where neither receives adequate technical attention.
- **Progression ladder:** Power clean → Hang clean → Squat clean from floor → Clean & Jerk complex. Jerk separately: push press (strength) → push jerk → split jerk.
- **Technical bottleneck most common at this level — the front rack:** An inadequate front rack forces the elbows down during the clean receiving position, which collapses the front squat position and compromises the jerk dip. Wrist and shoulder flexibility must be addressed directly (see `mobility_requirements`), not worked around by accepting a low elbow position.
- **The jerk-specific constraint:** The jerk dip must be vertical — any forward lean during the dip converts vertical drive into horizontal displacement of the bar. This is a motor control error that must be identified and corrected before increasing jerk load.
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 3 days
- **Intensity range:** Technical work: 65–80% 1RM; heavy singles: 85–95% when positions are reliable

---

### Pulls (Snatch Pull and Clean Pull)

- **Why this system requires this category:** The pull is the mechanical foundation of both lifts. Every repetition of a snatch or clean begins with a pull; improving the pull — specifically, achieving full triple extension before the bar is redirected — improves both lifts simultaneously without requiring a catch.
- **Loading principle — phase-specific:** Pull intensity is periodized across training blocks, not fixed. During accumulation phases, 95–105% optimizes bar path accuracy and speed-strength; the goal is volume accumulation with perfect mechanics. During intensification phases, 110–120% is required to maximize peak vertical ground reaction force (F_z) and overload the spinal extensors — this stimulus cannot be achieved at lower loads. Load increases in the 110–120% range are only valid if bar path quality is maintained; if the bar drifts or hip rise accelerates, reduce to 105% and rebuild.
- **The bar path standard applies at all intensities:** A pull that deviates from the lift's bar path — at any load — is training a competing motor pattern. This applies equally at 95% and 115%.
- **What the pull trains that the lift cannot:** Because there is no catch, the athlete can focus entirely on maintaining bar contact through the transition, achieving complete triple extension, and controlling the rate of hip rise in the first pull. These mechanics cannot be isolated when simultaneously managing the receiving position.
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 3 days (snatch pulls and clean pulls distributed across the week, not always both on the same day)
- **Intensity range:** Accumulation/Peaking: 95–105% of the corresponding lift 1RM; Intensification: 110–120%; sets of 3–4

---

### Front Squat

- **Why this system requires this category:** The front squat determines the ceiling of the clean receiving position. An athlete cannot receive more in the clean than they can front squat. Because the front rack position is identical to the clean receiving position, every front squat rep reinforces — or fails to reinforce — the correct receiving posture.
- **Why front squat takes priority over back squat:** The back squat develops general leg strength but does not train the front rack position, the upright torso requirement, or the specific knee tracking demanded by the clean receiving position. At the intermediate level, limited training time is better allocated to front squat.
- **Loading principle:** Front squat should be trained at 120–130% of the clean 1RM as a long-term target. If the front squat is below this ratio, the clean is being limited by receiving strength, not pulling strength.
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 2–3 days
- **Intensity range:** 75–90% of front squat 1RM; sets of 2–5

---

### Overhead Squat

- **Why this system requires this category:** The overhead squat is the snatch receiving position under load. It trains shoulder stability, thoracic extension, and hip flexibility simultaneously — all of which degrade under load if not specifically maintained.
- **Its role as a diagnostic:** A deterioration in overhead squat quality (weight or positional) at a load previously manageable is an early warning sign that the snatch receiving position will fail under heavier loads. Check overhead squat weekly as a diagnostic, not only as accessory strength work.
- **Mobility threshold:** If the overhead squat cannot be loaded to at least 80% of snatch 1RM with consistent receiving position, mobility is limiting the snatch, not strength. Address the restriction directly (see `mobility_requirements`).
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 2 days
- **Intensity range:** 75–85% of snatch 1RM; sets of 3–5

---

## Milestones

### Technical Indicators

- Squat snatch receiving position is below parallel consistently (not occasionally) — the bar does not "drop" into depth; the athlete actively pulls under
- In the clean receiving position, elbows are visibly above parallel in the front rack on every rep; no rep is accepted with dropped elbows
- Snatch bar path stays within 5 cm of the body through the transition from first to second pull — assessed visually from the side
- Split jerk receiving position is stable for a minimum 2-second hold on every rep; no press-out to lockout

### Strength / Fitness Indicators

- Snatch ≥ 75% of bodyweight
- Clean & Jerk ≥ 90% of bodyweight
- Front Squat ≥ 120% of bodyweight (at minimum; 130% is the target)
- Overhead Squat ≥ 75% of snatch 1RM

**Signal to advance to Advanced level:**
All four strength indicators met, snatch and clean & jerk receivable at depth in every training session without positional cueing, and the power snatch is ≤ 85% of squat snatch 1RM. A smaller gap (power snatch approaching 90–95% of squat snatch) indicates power-dependency — the athlete cannot receive at depth and relies on pulling the bar maximally high. A larger gap indicates growing technical efficiency: the athlete is pulling under the bar rather than over it. This is the correct direction of development.

---

## Common Violations

| Violation | Root Cause | Correct Approach |
|-----------|-----------|-----------------|
| Continuing to attempt a weight after 2 misses | Persistence confused with productive training | After 2 misses at a weight, reduce to a weight that can be made and end the session; missed lifts reinforce incorrect motor patterns |
| Replacing front squat with back squat | Back squat feels more productive due to higher loads | Front squat is non-negotiable for clean receiving strength development; back squat is supplementary, not a substitute |
| Neglecting overhead squat because it feels easy at low weights | The exercise is not challenging at current snatch loads | The overhead squat is a diagnostic; train it at 75–85% of snatch 1RM to maintain positional integrity under load |
| Training both snatch and clean & jerk at heavy singles on the same day | Attempting to maximize training density | Heavy snatch + heavy clean & jerk in the same session means one lift receives degraded technical attention; separate them or designate one as heavy and one as technical |
| Increasing load because a lift "looked good" on video | Confusing technical quality on a single rep with stable skill acquisition | Multiple sessions at the same load with consistent positions is the threshold for advancement, not a single impressive lift |
| Treating pulls as optional accessory work | Prioritizing time on the competition lifts themselves | Pulls are the most efficient way to improve the pull without accumulating catch-related fatigue; they should be treated as primary training, not optional extras |

# Powerlifting Advanced System Guide

## System Definition

**Goal:**
Maximize competition total on a planned meet date through block periodization or conjugate organization, using autoregulation (RPE/RIR) rather than fixed percentage loading, and managing the severely compressed MEV–MRV margin that characterizes advanced lifters. The central task at the advanced level is engineering the precise timing of peak fitness expression — the realization block and competition taper — because random or undefined peaking is the primary cause of underperformance at this level.

**Anti-goals:**
- Not running standard 5/3/1 or wave-loading indefinitely — simple weekly waves stop producing gains because the absolute rate of adaptation has slowed to the point where concentrated block stimuli are required to disrupt homeostasis adequately
- Not using fixed percentage loading — an advanced lifter's daily biological readiness fluctuates ±5–10%; rigid percentage prescription turns a planned RPE 8 into a genuine RPE 10 on a bad day, generating missed reps and disproportionate fatigue; autoregulation resolves this
- Not training without a competition date — advanced programming is built backward from a competition date; training without a defined peaking target produces unstructured accumulation with no defined realization phase
- Not maximizing training PRs — absolute maximal singles in the gym generate 7–14 days of neurological recovery debt that disrupts subsequent volume blocks; all in-gym PRs are repetition PRs or RPE 9 submaximal singles; true maximal expression belongs on the platform

---

## Weekly Structure Logic

| Work Type | Weekly Frequency | Core Reason |
|-----------|-----------------|-------------|
| Squat (competition + variations) | 2–3 days | Advanced squat volumes (10–14 working sets/week) require distribution to maintain quality; >8 sets/session degrades technique under high absolute loads |
| Bench press (competition + variations) | 3–4 days | Bench responds well to high frequency due to smaller muscle mass; distributing 12–18 weekly sets across 3–4 sessions maintains bar velocity and technical quality |
| Deadlift (heavy) | 1 day | Deadlift axial fatigue remains the primary limiter even at advanced level; 1 heavy session + 1 optional light/speed session is the ceiling |
| Deadlift (light/speed) | 0–1 day | Optional second deadlift session at 60–70% 1RM with focus on bar speed; does not exceed 5 working sets |
| Max-Effort (ME) work (conjugate) | 1–2 days | Near-maximal or supramaximal work on competition lift variations; prevents accommodation while building absolute recruitment capacity |
| Dynamic-Effort (DE) work (conjugate) | 1–2 days | 40–60% 1RM + 33% accommodating resistance, performed with maximal concentric velocity; develops rate of force development (RFD) |
| Accessory and synergist work | Each session (after main lift) | At advanced loads, synergist bottlenecks are more performance-limiting than at intermediate level |

**Structural Constraints (hard rules):**

- **Realization (peaking) block is 2–3 weeks, not 4+ weeks** — volume reduction during peaking is structurally detrained; if a peak exceeds 3–4 weeks, work capacity and muscle cross-section begin to atrophy, and the athlete arrives recovered but structurally weaker
- **Autoregulation via RPE is mandatory: no rigid percentage loading** — advanced lifters use a top set prescribed at RPE 8 (2 RIR) and adjust load based on daily readiness; the load on the bar varies session-to-session; the perceived effort is constant
- **ME day exercise rotation is required in conjugate: new variation every 1–3 weeks** — the Law of Accommodation causes rapid adaptation to repeated maximal stimuli; rotating ME variations prevents neural accommodation while maintaining the recruitment quality of near-maximal loading
- **DE sets require accommodating resistance providing ≥15% of total load in bands or chains** — submaximal bar speed training without accommodating resistance does not sufficiently alter the force-velocity profile; bands and chains are required to eliminate the deceleration phase and force continuous bar acceleration
- **Competition attempts must be selected before the meet: opener at 90–92% of projected 1RM** — opener is a weight the athlete can lift for 3 reps under any biological conditions; it must go up reliably on a bad day; openers at >92% of projected 1RM risk missed openers and psychological momentum loss for subsequent attempts

---

## Load Decision Framework

**Block periodization (12–16 week competition prep cycle):**

| Block | Duration | Intensity | Volume | Training Character |
|-------|----------|-----------|--------|-------------------|
| Accumulation | 4–6 weeks | 60–75% 1RM | High (10–14 sets/lift/week) | Hypertrophy and work capacity base; moderate loads, high rep ranges (4–6 reps) |
| Transmutation | 4–5 weeks | 75–90% 1RM | Moderate (8–12 sets/lift/week) | Strength conversion; heavy loads, lower rep ranges (2–4 reps) |
| Realization | 2–3 weeks | 90%+ 1RM | Low (4–6 sets/lift/week) | Fatigue dissipation + strength expression; singles and doubles, maximal intent |

**Autoregulation decision rules:**
- Prescribed: "Top set of 5 at RPE 8" → select the load that allows 5 reps with exactly 2 RIR
- If bar speed is unusually fast and RPE is lower than expected: add weight; today is a high-readiness day
- If bar speed is unusually slow and RPE is higher than expected: maintain load or reduce slightly; today is a low-readiness day; do not force prescribed loads

**Competition attempt selection:**
- **Opener (Attempt 1):** 90–92% of projected 1RM; RPE 7.5–8.5; a weight the athlete can perform for 3 reps under any conditions
- **Attempt 2:** 95–97% of projected 1RM; RPE ~9; a weight that secures a competitive total
- **Attempt 3:** 100–102% of projected 1RM; RPE 10; maximal expression under optimal meet-day conditions

**Fatigue management:**
Mandate deload when ≥2 of the following appear across 3 consecutive sessions:
- Standard submaximal sets (70–80%) rating 1–1.5 RPE higher than baseline
- Persistent "flat" feeling: no explosive drive, warm-up bar feels heavy
- Acute, non-specific joint aching or tendon flare-up (patellar, triceps)
- 15% grip strength decline vs. baseline (hand dynamometer if available)
- Consistent technical breakdown on loads that were clean in previous weeks

---

## Movement Category Definitions

### Competition Squat (Max-Effort and Dynamic-Effort)

- **Why this system requires this category at the advanced level:** Standard working sets at 80–85% are insufficient to disrupt a highly trained neuromuscular system; ME and DE sessions provide qualitatively different stimuli that maintain progress when standard loading produces accommodation.
- **Max-Effort squat variations:** Safety squat bar (SSB) squat, box squat (parallel or below), front squat, low-bar paused squat; rotate variation every 1–3 weeks; always work to a 1RM or 3RM maximum on ME day
- **Dynamic-Effort squat:** Competition low-bar at 40–60% 1RM + 33% in bands/chains; 8–10 sets of 2 reps with maximal concentric intent; rest 45–60 seconds between sets; the goal is bar speed, not fatigue
- **Box squat mechanics:** Breaking the stretch-shortening cycle at the box forces a purely concentric drive, developing the glutes and hip extensors at the critical low-position; combined with band tension that increases toward lockout, it addresses the sticking point specific to advanced squatters
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 2 sessions (1 ME or heavy, 1 DE or volume work)
- **Volume range:** 10–14 working sets/week

---

### Competition Bench Press (Max-Effort and Dynamic-Effort)

- **Why this system requires this category:** Bench press is the lift most responsive to high frequency; advanced lifters often bench 3–4x/week with different variations targeting distinct weak points.
- **Max-Effort bench variations:** Board press (2, 3, or 4 board), close-grip bench, floor press, slingshot or specialty bar bench; board press allows supramaximal loads (>100% 1RM) to overload the lockout; floor press eliminates leg drive and shoulder extension, isolating chest/triceps
- **Dynamic-Effort bench:** 40–60% 1RM + accommodating resistance; 8–10 sets of 3 reps with maximal concentric speed; focus on bar path consistency and explosive drive off the chest
- **Advanced bench weak-point targeting:**
  - Weak off the chest → paused bench, Spoto press, floor press
  - Weak at mid-range → board press (1 board, limiting ROM to mid-range), triceps volume
  - Weak at lockout → board press (3–4 boards), close-grip bench
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 3–4 sessions
- **Volume range:** 12–18 working sets/week

---

### Competition Deadlift (Heavy and Speed)

- **Why this system requires this category:** Despite low frequency, the deadlift requires the highest technical specificity of the three lifts — bar drift, hips rising first, and lockout hyperextension all cost attempts on meet day, and they cannot be corrected at competition; they must be rehearsed correctly every session.
- **Heavy deadlift:** Competition stance, 80–90%+ 1RM; 1–3 reps per set; 5–8 working sets/week
- **Speed deadlift (optional second session):** 55–65% 1RM, performed with maximal intent; 5 sets of 2–3 reps; develops rate of force development off the floor without adding CNS load
- **Accessory selection at advanced level:** Rack pulls become more important as sticking point shifts to lockout; deficit deadlifts address floor-pull weakness; good mornings and Romanian deadlifts maintain posterior chain hypertrophy when heavy deadlift frequency is restricted
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 1 heavy session; 1 optional speed session
- **Volume range:** 5–8 heavy working sets/week

---

### Accessory Work (Advanced Role)

At the advanced level, accessory work becomes more precisely targeted — it directly addresses the specific mechanical bottleneck limiting the competition 1RM rather than providing general synergist development.

- **Squat accessories (advanced):** SSB squat (thoracic collapse correction), tempo goblet squat (hip mobility under load), single-leg work if hip shift is present
- **Bench accessories (advanced):** Heavy triceps work (JM press, close-grip, weighted dips) if lockout fails; lat strengthening (weighted pull-ups, seal rows) if the upper back loses tension at the bottom
- **Deadlift accessories (advanced):** Snatch-grip deadlift (upper back bracing under extreme width), block pulls (bar position confidence at knee height), Romanian deadlift for hamstring hypertrophy when heavy pulling frequency is limited

---

## Milestones

### Technical Indicators

- Competition squat, bench, and deadlift all executable to federation standard under simulated meet conditions (commands, timing, depth and pause protocols)
- Full warm-up sequence completed and peak performance achieved within a 10-minute warm-up window (meet timing requirement)
- Meet-day attempt selection consistent with RPE predictions from training (opener goes up at predicted RPE; no missed openers in last 3 meets)

### Strength Indicators

These are approximate standards for a drug-tested, raw, male advanced lifter (~83 kg bodyweight), representing a DOTS score of approximately 474:

| Lift | Standard |
|------|----------|
| Squat | 220 kg (2.65× BW) |
| Bench press | 155 kg (1.87× BW) |
| Deadlift | 265 kg (3.19× BW) |
| Total | 640 kg |

## Common Violations

| Violation | Root Cause | Correct Approach |
|-----------|-----------|-----------------|
| Peaking for >4 weeks | Believing more recovery time produces more strength | Volume reduction during peaking is structural detraining; after 3–4 weeks, muscle cross-section and work capacity begin to atrophy; the athlete arrives recovered but weaker |
| Using fixed percentages at advanced level | Assuming performance is consistent day-to-day | Advanced lifters' biological readiness fluctuates ±5–10%; a rigid 85% session on a low-readiness day shifts to RPE 10 and generates missed reps; autoregulation via RPE ensures the intended stimulus regardless of daily variation |
| Grinding RPE 10 gym singles regularly | Treating gym PRs as meaningful strength milestones | RPE 10 efforts deplete neurotransmitter pools for 7–14 days; this window represents weeks of productive volume lost; all in-gym PRs should come from rep PRs or RPE 9 singles |
| Rotating conjugate variations too frequently | Not understanding that ME day requires periodic max effort to know if the variation is progressing | Rotating ME variations more often than every 1–3 weeks prevents establishing a performance baseline for each variation; the system fails to reveal whether progress is being made |
| Water cut exceeding 3% body weight for a 2-hour weigh-in | Attempting to compete in a lower weight class without a 24-hour window | A water cut >3% BW in a 2-hour window causes severe intracellular dehydration and plasma volume depletion; force production drops measurably and cramping is common; cuts >3% require a 24-hour weigh-in window for safe rehydration |
| Not competing to peak meets on a fixed annual schedule | Training without a competitive date forces indefinite accumulation | Without a fixed competition date, there is no realization phase; the lifter accumulates fitness but never expresses it; advanced periodization requires at least 1–2 competition targets per year to structure blocks meaningfully |

# Powerlifting Intermediate System Guide

## System Definition

**Goal:**
Develop maximal 1RM strength in the squat, bench press, and deadlift through a monthly or multi-week wave-loading progression, transitioning from the session-to-session linear model that has been exhausted at the novice stage. The central task at the intermediate level is extending the stress-recovery-adaptation (SRA) cycle to fit a full weekly or 4-week microcycle, because absolute loads are now high enough that fatigue cannot clear within 48–72 hours.

**Anti-goals:**
- Not treating training as bodybuilding — muscle hypertrophy is a means to an end (more cross-section = more force capacity), but isolation work and rep ranges above 8 are accessories, not primary training
- Not grinding near-maximal singles in every session — lifts taking >5 seconds to pass a sticking point drain neurotransmitter capacity for 7–14 days, consuming recovery budget required for productive subsequent volume blocks; save absolute maximal efforts for the competition platform
- Not neglecting accessory work — the main lifts stress prime movers exclusively; synergists (gluteus medius, rotator cuff, lower traps, brachialis) lag behind and become mechanical bottlenecks if not directly trained
- Not avoiding competition — there is no absolute strength prerequisite for a first meet; platform experience is the only way to develop meet-day skill, and attempting to reach arbitrary gym numbers before competing delays the development of competition-specific competence

---

## Weekly Structure Logic

| Work Type | Weekly Frequency | Core Reason |
|-----------|-----------------|-------------|
| Squat (primary or variation) | 2 days | 2x/week frequency is superior to 1x for 1RM development in intermediates; distributes volume without exceeding recoverable per-session demands |
| Bench press (primary or variation) | 2–3 days | Bench responds well to higher frequency due to smaller muscle mass and shorter ROM; 2–3x distributes the necessary 8–12 weekly working sets without shoulder fatigue accumulation |
| Deadlift | 1 day | Deadlift generates the highest axial loading and neurological fatigue footprint of the three lifts; 1x/week is optimal — adding a second heavy session produces chronic lower back fatigue faster than adaptation |
| Competition lift technique practice | Each session | Technical consistency must be built on the exact competition movement pattern; training with close variants is additive, not substitutive |
| Accessory work (squat, bench, deadlift specific) | 2–3 days | Addresses mechanical bottlenecks in each lift; always follows the main lift it supports |
| Mandatory rest | 3–4 days | Intermediate lifters frequently undertrain relative to what they believe; CNS fatigue from the main lifts is non-local and affects every subsequent session |

**Structural Constraints (hard rules):**

- **The deadlift is trained on the last heavy session of the week** — deadlift generates the deepest systemic fatigue of the three lifts; placing it first or in the middle compromises squat and bench quality in subsequent sessions
- **No consecutive training days for the same lift** — the SRA cycle for intermediate powerlifters requires a minimum of 48–72 hours between sessions for the same movement pattern; consecutive days prevent full expression of the supercompensation peak
- **The Training Max (TM) in 5/3/1 must be set at 85–90% of true 1RM, not 100%** — setting TM at true 1RM shifts every prescribed percentage into near-maximal, exhausting zones; within 4–6 weeks, AMRAP sets consistently fail, generating massive neural fatigue and halting progression
- **Competition lift technique is non-negotiable on main work sets** — paused bench press if competing touch-and-go is not allowed by your federation; depth standard met on every rep in training; training around competition rules builds the specificity required for carryover

---

## Load Decision Framework

**5/3/1 progression rules:**
- **Increase TM:** When the "1+" AMRAP set in Week 3 produces ≥ 5 clean reps with maintained technique, increase TM for the next 4-week cycle (+2.5 kg for bench/OHP, +5 kg for squat/deadlift)
- **Hold TM:** When the AMRAP set produces 3–4 reps; re-run the cycle at the same TM
- **Reduce TM 10%:** When the AMRAP set produces <3 reps or when technical breakdown occurs (knee cave, hips rising, bar path deviation); rebuild from a lower base

**Texas Method progression rules:**
- **Volume Day → Intensity Day cycle:** If Friday intensity day produces a clean new 5RM PR, the following Monday's volume day load increases proportionally (typically 2.5–5 kg)
- **When no PR is achieved on Intensity Day:** Do not increase Monday volume day load; assess recovery markers; consider reducing Wednesday recovery day load further

**Fatigue management — when to mandate a deload:**
Mandate a deload week when the athlete presents with two or more of the following markers across three consecutive sessions:
- Warm-up sets at 70–80% 1RM rating 1–1.5 RPE points higher than baseline
- Bar velocity noticeably slower than previous sessions at the same load
- Persistent, non-specific joint aching in knees, elbows, or lower back
- Resting heart rate elevated >7 bpm above baseline for 3 consecutive mornings
- A session feels subjectively heavier than the previous one at the same prescribed load

**Volume vs. intensity adjustment decision:**
- **Reduce volume** if: chronically slow bar speed, systemic lethargy, unable to recover between sessions, but joints feel stable — volume is accumulating fatigue faster than it can clear
- **Reduce intensity** if: acute joint pain, form collapse on sub-maximal working sets, consistently failing to lockout reps that should be submaximal — intensity is exceeding structural tolerance; build work capacity at lower intensities first

**In-session missed rep protocol:**
- If a rep is missed or RPE exceeds 9.5 on a working set: reduce load 5–10% for remaining sets of that session; do not attempt to complete the prescribed sets at the original load
- If stagnation persists for two consecutive weeks: execute a program reset — reduce TM by 10–15% and rebuild

---

## Movement Category Definitions

### Squat Pattern

- **Why this system requires this category:** The squat is the first of the three competition lifts and the largest lower-body strength driver. Every program decision downstream of the squat — deadlift frequency, accessory selection — is conditioned by the fatigue and technical requirements of heavy squatting.
- **What this typically means in this system:** Competition low-bar back squat (primary), with paused squat, front squat, or safety squat bar as accessories
- **Selection criteria for competition squat:** Bar placed on the spine of the scapula on the shelf created by the rear deltoids (low-bar); stance width sufficient to clear the torso through full depth; hip crease must pass below top of knee per IPF standards
- **Low-bar vs. high-bar mechanical difference:** Low-bar shortens the distance from bar to hip joint, allowing greater forward lean while keeping bar over midfoot; this lengthens the hip moment arm and shifts load to the posterior chain (glutes, hamstrings, adductors); high-bar maximizes quad recruitment but is biomechanically less efficient for most lifters in the competition low-bar
- **Accessory rationale:**
  - Paused squat: eliminates stored elastic energy at the bottom, building positional strength out of the hole
  - Front squat: maximizes knee joint moment and quad recruitment while reducing spinal load; trains the upper back for bar position stability
  - SSB squat: camber shifts weight forward, forcing thoracic extensors — useful when forward lean is the primary limiting factor
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 2 sessions
- **Intensity range:** Main work: 75–85% 1RM for working sets; top set at RPE 8 in autoregulated models

---

### Horizontal Push (Bench Press)

- **Why this system requires this category:** The bench press is the second competition lift and the primary upper body strength expression. Unlike in bodybuilding, technical optimization — arch mechanics, leg drive, bar path — takes precedence over muscle isolation.
- **What this typically means in this system:** Competition paused bench press (primary), close-grip bench (triceps lockout strength), board press or floor press (range restriction for sticking point overload)
- **Selection criteria for competition bench:** Grip at or approaching the legal limit (81 cm, or as wide as is comfortable with legal contact points); legal arch that contacts all required body parts; the bar descends in a J-curve (touching lower chest, then pressing up and back toward the rack)
- **Arch mechanics:** The arch reduces bar travel distance by elevating the touchpoint; the incline created by the arch recruits sternocostal pec fibers more efficiently; leg drive transfers horizontal force through the body to create a rigid platform (push the floor toward the feet, not the ceiling toward the plates)
- **Paused vs. touch-and-go:** Competition requires a pause; training must reflect this — the pause removes stored elastic energy and trains starting strength from a dead stop; athletes who train touch-and-go consistently will have no starting strength when the judge gives the press command
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 2–3 sessions
- **Intensity range:** Main work: 75–85% 1RM; close-grip and accessories: 65–80%

---

### Hip Hinge Pull (Deadlift)

- **Why this system requires this category:** The deadlift is the third competition lift and the highest absolute load movement in powerlifting. Its neurological fatigue footprint is disproportionately large relative to the local muscle damage, which is why frequency is capped at 1x/week for intermediates.
- **What this typically means in this system:** Conventional or sumo deadlift (competition stance), deficit deadlift (off-the-floor strength), rack pull (lockout and upper back overloading)
- **Conventional vs. sumo selection:** The choice is anthropometry-dependent, not universal. Lifters with long arms relative to height — who do not have to hinge deeply to grip the bar — tend to favor conventional. Lifters with longer femurs relative to torso often find sumo reduces spinal shear (10–20% lower horizontal force on the lumbar spine) and allows better positioning. Both are legal; use the style that allows the most weight with the best technique.
- **Sticking point:** The primary deadlift sticking point is just above the knee, where the quadriceps contribution ends and the hip extensors must work against maximum moment arm to complete the hinge. Deficit deadlifts address weakness off the floor; rack pulls address weakness at lockout.
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 1 session (heavy)
- **Intensity range:** Competition sets: 80–90% 1RM; accessories: 65–80%

---

### Accessory Work

- **Why this system requires this category:** The three competition lifts create specific mechanical bottlenecks as prime movers outpace stabilizing synergists. Without targeted accessory work, these imbalances become hard limiters on 1RM — not because the prime mover is weak, but because a synergist is failing.
- **Primary squat accessories:** Paused squat (positional control out of the hole), front squat (upper back and quad strength), SSB squat (thoracic extension under load)
- **Primary bench accessories:** Close-grip bench (triceps lockout), board press or floor press (sticking point and lockout overload with supramaximal loads), paused bench at various heights (lat engagement and stability at different positions)
- **Primary deadlift accessories:** Deficit deadlift (off-the-floor quad and glute power), rack pull (grip, upper back, and lockout), Romanian deadlift (hamstring and hip hinge capacity)
- **Upper back accessory (applies to all three lifts):** Barbell row or chest-supported row — upper back weakness is a universal bottleneck in intermediate powerlifting; it causes bench press technical collapse, bar rollout in the squat, and bar drift in the deadlift
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 2–3 sessions (woven after main lift work)

---

## Milestones

### Technical Indicators

- Squat: consistent depth (hip crease below knee top) on every training rep with maintained neutral spine; no hip shift or bar rollout on working sets
- Bench: legal pause with full control every rep; J-curve bar path habitual; leg drive present without glute lift-off
- Deadlift: bar remains in contact with the shins throughout the pull; no hips-first movement off the floor; full lockout without hyperextension

### Strength Indicators

These are approximate standards for a drug-tested, raw, male intermediate lifter (~83 kg bodyweight), representing a DOTS score of approximately 388:

| Lift | Standard |
|------|----------|
| Squat | 180 kg (2.17× BW) |
| Bench press | 125 kg (1.50× BW) |
| Deadlift | 220 kg (2.65× BW) |
| Total | 525 kg |

**Signal to advance to Advanced level:**
Monthly progression has stalled across two or more consecutive cycles despite optimal nutrition and recovery; TM resets no longer produce renewed progress; the athlete has competed in multiple meets and requires coach-guided periodization to continue progress.

## Common Violations

| Violation | Root Cause | Correct Approach |
|-----------|-----------|-----------------|
| Setting 5/3/1 Training Max at 100% of true 1RM | Believing the TM should reflect actual strength | TM at 100% shifts all prescribed percentages into near-maximal zones; within 4–6 weeks, AMRAP sets fail consistently and systemic fatigue halts progression; TM must be 85–90% of true 1RM |
| Grinding >5-second singles in training regularly | Treating gym PRs as equivalent to competition PRs | Lifts taking >5 seconds drain the neurotransmitter pool (acetylcholine, dopamine) for 7–14 days; reserve absolute max efforts for competition; all gym PRs should come from rep PRs or RPE 9 singles |
| Deadlifting 2x/week heavy | Assuming more frequency always means more strength | Deadlift axial loading generates disproportionate spinal fatigue; >2 heavy deadlift sessions/week does not allow deep spinal stabilizers and CNS to recover; frequency for the deadlift should be lower than for squat and bench |
| Training touch-and-go bench when competing under pause rules | Not matching training specificity to competition standard | The stretch-shortening cycle provides 2–7% carryover; removing it in competition and having no paused training means no starting strength from a dead stop; miss openers become very likely |
| Skipping competition indefinitely waiting for "strong enough" | Believing platform experience can be replaced by gym numbers | There is no strength prerequisite for a first meet; the psychological and tactical skills of competition can only be learned on the platform; first meets are always educational regardless of total |
| Deadlifting on Day 1 of the training week | Treating the deadlift as the "king lift" that should go first | Deadlift fatigue is systemic, not local; placing it at the start means every subsequent session in the week trains in its fatigue shadow; the deadlift belongs at the end of the training week |

# Strongman Advanced System Guide

## System Definition

**Goal:**
With a specific competition date as the endpoint, use block periodization to converge all training capacity toward maximum performance on competition-standard implements at the competition venue. The essence of advanced strongman training is **competition simulation fidelity**: the final phase of training does not make you stronger — it ensures that the strength you already have is fully expressed on competition day.

**Anti-goals:**
- Not pursuing strength on non-competition implements — every training decision at the advanced level must answer the question: "which competition event does this directly serve?"
- Not maximizing training volume — advanced athletes accumulate fatigue far faster than intermediate athletes; overreaching is the most common cause of regression at this level
- Not targeting body weight outside of the weight class — in advanced strongman, muscle mass that exceeds the weight class limit is weight you have to carry onto the scale
- Not chasing powerlifting-style 1RM peaks — advanced strongman competition demands multi-event sequential output, not isolated single-effort maximum strength

---

## Weekly Structure Logic

| Work Type | Weekly Frequency | Core Reason |
|-----------|-----------------|-------------|
| Barbell strength base (lower body) | 1–2 days | Maintain deadlift and squat base; reduce to 1 day in competition prep |
| Barbell strength base (upper body) | 1 day | Overhead strength maintenance; continuous increase is no longer the goal |
| Competition implement simulation days | 2–3 days | At the advanced level, competition implements must occupy the majority of training time |
| Mandatory rest | ≥ 2 days | Neural recovery; training intensity at the advanced level makes 2 days the minimum threshold |

**Structural Constraints (hard rules):**

- **From 8 weeks out, all competition events must be practiced on competition-standard implements, at competition distances and weights** — substitutes are no longer sufficient at this stage
- **No new implements or training methods introduced in the final 4 weeks** — neural adaptation takes time; introducing novelty before competition creates uncertainty, not readiness
- **Each competition event is fully simulated at least once per week** — not a "feel" rehearsal, but a complete single-attempt at competition standard
- **Taper begins 2 weeks out:** volume reduced by 50%, intensity reduced to ≈50% of peak in the final week (intensity does not hold — it drops with volume); this is not rest — it is simultaneous fatigue elimination and intensity reduction to allow full neuromuscular restoration
- **Training ceases 3–4 days before competition** — complete cessation in the final 3–4 days is research-supported (Winwood 2018); no barbell work, no implements, no "just to stay sharp" sessions

---

## Load Decision Framework

**When to increase load:**
Barbell days — only when certain that training quality can be maintained at the same level for the next 3 weeks. Load increases at the advanced level are quarterly decisions, not weekly ones. Event days — only when the same implement load on the same event feels "in control" (not barely completed) across two consecutive sessions.

**When to reduce load (the most important skill at the advanced level):**
- Competition implement performance declines for two consecutive weeks → immediately reduce load for one week; do not attempt again
- Morning resting heart rate elevated > 7 bpm above baseline for 3 consecutive days → reduce total training load; see `overtraining_signs`
- Any competition movement "feels wrong" → reduce weight and work on technique; push through in competition, not in training
- 4 weeks before competition, actively reduce load regardless of how good things feel — this is part of the plan, not a concession

**Pre-competition taper logic:**
- Week 15 (the week before competition week): volume −50%, intensity reduced to ≈50% of peak (not held at 85–90%; both volume and intensity drop simultaneously in the final week per Winwood 2018)
- Competition week: Monday — technique confirmation only at ≤50% of competition weight (single reps, no effort work); training ceases Tuesday–Thursday; Competition day Saturday

---

## Movement Category Definitions

### Competition Overhead Events

- **What this system requires at the advanced level:** Not merely the ability to lift the implement, but the ability to complete the required reps or maximum weight on the competition floor under accumulated fatigue from preceding events
- **What this means in this system:** Log press (highest priority), Axle press, Circus dumbbell (if specified by the event)
- **Selection criteria:** Exact replication of competition implements — diameter, grip width, weight. The log used in training must match the competition log diameter.
- **Non-substitutability:** At the advanced level, there is no adequate substitute for a log. The log's center of mass sits outside the bar, fundamentally altering the entire movement pattern.
- **Periodization design:** General preparation phase may use barbell; from 8 weeks out, switch to 100% log/axle; competition week — technique confirmation at ≤50% of competition weight only
- **Opening attempt selection principle:** Select a weight that has been confidently completed in each of the last 3 training sessions, not the best single performance

---

### Competition Deadlift and Pull Events

- **What this system requires at the advanced level:** Most competition deadlift events are "maximum reps in 60 seconds," not "maximum single weight" — this is an entirely different training stimulus
- **What this means in this system:** Competition deadlift (conventional or sumo per event rules), Frame carry, Axle deadlift
- **Selection criteria:** Use the grip width restrictions specified by the event (many competitions restrict foot placement); practice the competition rhythm (touch-and-go or dead stop per the rules)
- **Special principles for max-rep events:** Train in timed sets, not "feel sets"; set a training rep ceiling at 80% of the competition target — do not exhaust the full capacity in training
- **Intensity range:** Strength days: 85–95% 1RM, low reps; Event simulation days: 75–80% in timed sets

---

### Competition Carry Events

- **What this system requires at the advanced level:** Competition carry events must be executed while under accumulated fatigue from prior events — maintaining complete movement quality in a depleted state
- **What this means in this system:** Yoke walk (heaviest carry in competition), Farmer carry, Sandbag/Keg carry
- **Advanced Yoke rules:**
  - Overload training (5–10% above competition weight) is restricted to sets of 5–10 m only. At elite load levels — where competition Yoke may be 350–450 kg — a 5–10% overload walk of 20–25 m exposes the patellar tendons and lumbar facets to cumulative shear stress that significantly elevates injury risk. Short overload sets develop starting rigidity and neural adaptation to supramaximal weight; they are not intended for race-pace endurance.
  - Full 20+ m sets at race pace are performed at competition weight or below. Race-pace endurance is built at the actual competition load, not the overload load.
  - Competition weight must be completed at competition speed — not slow passage, but race pace with a consistent stride frequency target
  - Emergency set-down and repositioning must be practiced in training — never attempt these for the first time in competition
- **Carry protocol from 4 weeks out:** Every event day must include at least one complete competition carry event at competition standard distance
- **Distance range:** Competition standard distance (typically 20–25 m); occasional 1.5× distance overload in earlier training blocks

---

### Competition Loading Events (Atlas Stone / Object Load)

- **What this system requires at the advanced level:** Loading events have the steepest learning curve and the lowest transfer from other strength sports of any strongman discipline
- **What this means in this system:** Atlas stone series (standard competition), Keg toss, Car deadlift (if event-specified)
- **The mechanics of Atlas stone (required knowledge for AI course generation):**
  - First pull: roll the stone up the thighs, maintaining rounded back tension (this is the only strongman movement where deliberate upper-back rounding is correct)
  - Lap and load: stone is locked between thighs and abdomen; full-body extension "flips" the stone over the platform height
  - This is not a deadlift, and not a squat — Atlas stone must be trained with actual stones; substitutes (sandbags, etc.) are supplementary, not replacements
- **Tacky protocol (mandatory from Block 2 onward):** Tacky exponentially increases friction between the forearms and stone, shifting the primary force demand from forearm isometric squeeze to full posterior-chain hip drive. These are biomechanically distinct movements — training without tacky and competing with tacky means the neuromuscular pattern rehearsed in training does not match what the body executes on competition day. From 6–8 weeks out (entering Block 2), all Atlas stone training must use competition-standard tacky. Earlier blocks may train bare to develop base forearm strength, but this window must close before the specific preparation phase begins.
- **Advanced stone protocol:** At least one actual stone session per week; from 6 weeks out, practice with the full competition weight series using tacky throughout

---

### Barbell Strength Maintenance

- **What this system requires at the advanced level:** The role of barbell training shifts from "building" to "maintaining" at the advanced level
- **What this means in this system:** Conventional/Sumo deadlift, Overhead press, Back squat
- **Selection criteria:** Stick to fully mastered movements; do not experiment with new barbell technique at the advanced level
- **Volume principle:** Barbell work does not exceed 40% of total training volume; the remainder belongs to implements
- **Pre-competition reduction:** From 4 weeks out, reduce barbell days from 2 to 1 per week; from 2 weeks out, remove independent barbell days entirely

---

## Milestones

### Technical Indicators

- Complete log press at competition-specified weight for the required rep count, with stable receiving position and no excessive lumbar hyperextension as compensation
- Complete a 20 m Yoke walk at competition weight under the target contest time (≤ 10 seconds for most weight classes) with consistent step rhythm, stable stride frequency, and no significant lateral sway
- Load the heaviest stone in the competition series to the platform independently, without needing coaching cues during the attempt
- Performance gap between competition implement simulation in training and actual competition result is consistently within 5%

### Strength / Fitness Indicators

- Conventional Deadlift ≥ 2.5× bodyweight
- Log Press ≥ 1.0× bodyweight
- Back Squat ≥ 2.0× bodyweight
- Farmer carry: single-hand ≥ 0.7× bodyweight, 20 m unbroken

**Signal of reaching the top of the advanced tier:**
Consistently placing in the top 25% of the weight class, and the final event in a multi-event competition shows no significant technical breakdown relative to the first event.

---

## Common Violations

| Violation | Root Cause | Correct Approach |
|-----------|-----------|-----------------|
| Attempting new PRs the week before competition | Confusing training goals with competition goals | The final 2 weeks allow only technique confirmation at ≤50% of competition weight; PR attempts belong on the competition platform |
| Barbell training occupying more than 60% of training time | Applying powerlifting or weightlifting training logic to strongman | Advanced strongman allocates 60%+ of training time to implements; barbell is foundation maintenance, not the primary stimulus |
| Using sandbags as a full replacement for Atlas stone | No access to stones or logistical constraints | Sandbags are a useful supplement, not a substitute; stone contact must be established from 8 weeks out; first contact cannot happen on competition day |
| Continuing high-volume training during the pre-competition taper | Belief that maintaining volume prevents detraining | The taper's purpose is fatigue elimination, not fitness building; 50% volume reduction is supported by the evidence base (see `deload_protocol`) |
| Changing the opening attempt selection in competition week | Competition-week anxiety driving decisions | Opening attempts are fixed 3 weeks before competition; failed openers carry a psychological cost that far exceeds the physical cost of one missed lift |
| Ignoring the cumulative fitness demand of multi-event performance | Treating strongman as a strength sport rather than a competitive sport | From 6 weeks out, include at least one session per week simulating 3 or more consecutive events without rest; the fatigue pattern of competition is unique and must be trained |
| Randomizing event order in medley simulations | Treating all fatigue as interchangeable | Once the official contest script is published, medley simulations must replicate the exact event sequence — the specific pre-exhaustion pattern on each muscle group is what is being adapted to; reversing the order trains a different physiological state than competition day presents |

# Strongman Intermediate System Guide

## System Definition

**Goal:**
Build a barbell strength base sufficient to support competitive performance while developing implement-specific movement patterns — asymmetric loading, irregular object control, and multi-directional loaded movement. The central task at the intermediate level is teaching the body to maintain trunk rigidity under highly unstable external loads. This is the defining characteristic that separates strongman from every other strength sport.

**Anti-goals:**
- Not pursuing maximal single-joint hypertrophy — strongman implements demand full-body coordination; isolated muscle training transfers poorly
- Not pursuing Olympic weightlifting bar speed — speed is beneficial, but the priority at this stage is building slow, high-tension carrying capacity
- Not maximizing aerobic fitness — the metabolic demands of intermediate strongman are determined by event intervals, not sustained output
- Not chasing powerlifting peak 1RM — strength expression in strongman means moving with an implement, not a static barbell single

---

## Weekly Structure Logic

| Work Type | Weekly Frequency | Core Reason |
|-----------|-----------------|-------------|
| Barbell strength base (lower-body dominant) | 1 day | Provides a quantifiable progress baseline for deadlift and squat |
| Barbell strength base (upper-body dominant) | 1 day | Overhead strength is the most frequently tested single capacity in strongman events |
| Event day — pull-based (Frame carry, Farmer carry, Deadlift medley) | 1 day | Direct implement exposure for pulling events |
| Event day — press/carry (Log press, Yoke walk, Sandbag carry) | 1 day | Direct implement exposure for pressing and loading events |
| Mandatory rest | ≥ 3 days | Neural fatigue from event training far exceeds perceived fatigue; insufficient recovery is the most common cause of intermediate stagnation |

**Structural Constraints (hard rules):**

- **Yoke walk is placed only on the last training day of the week** — Yoke imposes the greatest axial spinal compression of any implement; placing it last preserves strength quality on all preceding sessions
- **Event days are not adjacent to the heaviest barbell day** — at least one full rest day between them; adjacent placement causes technical breakdown on the event day
- **Upper and lower barbell days are not consecutive** — shared neural drive; consecutive placement degrades quality on both days
- **Minimum 2 implement contacts per week** — neural adaptation to implement mechanics requires frequency; fewer than 2 contacts per week causes regression faster than adaptation
- **Never schedule 3 consecutive high-intensity training days** — accumulated fatigue, not the load itself, is the primary injury mechanism in strongman

---

## Load Decision Framework

**When to increase load:**
Barbell days — when all working sets are completed at the top of the target rep range with ≥ 2 RIR remaining on the final set, add load at the next session. Event days (pressing/loading) — when the same implement load is completed with stable receiving position and RPE < 8, add load. Event days (carries) — apply both criteria before adding weight: (1) technical: stable step rhythm, no sway, RPE < 8; (2) velocity: 20 m completed within a target time appropriate for the implement (as a reference, competition-pace Farmer carry targets ≤ 12 seconds for 20 m at working weight). An athlete who can cover 20 m but requires 25 seconds is under excessive load — technique is surviving, not functioning.

**When to reduce load:**
- Technical breakdown: trunk alignment lost, step rhythm disrupted, Yoke sway excessive → reduce load 10–15% immediately, do not wait until next week
- Resting heart rate elevated > 7 bpm above baseline for 3 consecutive days → reduce total load; see `overtraining_signs`
- An event session feels heavier than the previous one at the same load → this is a fatigue signal, not a weakness; reduce load for one week

**The volume-intensity balance principle:**
Intermediate strongman athletes almost always undertrain rather than overtrain, because the psychological barrier to implement training is high and perceived fatigue is vivid. Given technically sound movement, prioritize volume over intensity — 5 × 5 @ 80% produces better long-term results than 5 × 1 @ 95% at this level.

---

## Movement Category Definitions

### Overhead Pressing and Cleaning Pattern

- **Why this system requires this category:** Overhead is the most frequently appearing event category in strongman competitions — log press, axle press, and circus dumbbell all belong here. Without sufficient overhead strength, no other capacity can be fully expressed on the competition floor.
- **What this typically means in this system:** Log press (preferred), Axle press, Barbell strict press (as a strength foundation tool, not a competition movement)
- **Selection criteria:** Heavy axial loading, grip width that forces shoulder girdle stability rather than barbell-optimized mechanics, allows leg drive (push press)
- **Why the standard barbell is not the primary implement:** There are no standard barbell overhead events in sanctioned strongman; the grip width and center of mass of a log or axle differ significantly from a straight barbell, limiting transfer
- **Critical technical sub-component — the Clean:** Many intermediate athletes fail overhead events not because they cannot press the weight, but because they cannot clean it to the rack position. This is especially acute with the Axle Bar, which requires the Continental Clean — a two-stage lap-and-rack technique that rests the bar on the stomach between pulls. The Continental Clean has no equivalent in Olympic lifting and must be practiced as a separate technical skill. If the clean becomes the limiting factor, isolate it into dedicated technical volume before treating overhead as a strength problem.
- **Training structure implication:** Overhead sessions must include clean practice, not just pressing. At minimum: 3–4 clean-only sets to the rack position before any pressing work.
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 2 (1 barbell strength day + 1 event day)
- **Intensity range:** Barbell day: 75–85% 1RM; Event day: 70–80% of implement maximum

---

### Hip Hinge Pull

- **Why this system requires this category:** Deadlift, Frame carry, Farmer carry, and Atlas stone all depend on the posterior chain maintaining tension under high load. This is the highest-density movement pattern in strongman events.
- **What this typically means in this system:** Conventional deadlift (strength foundation), Sumo deadlift (axle deadlift event substitute), Farmer carry (event day primary)
- **Selection criteria:** Full-range posterior chain tension development, accommodates varied grip widths and stances to simulate different implements
- **Why the Romanian deadlift is not the primary movement:** RDL is eccentric-dominant, but strongman pulling events are concentric-initiated; useful as a supplementary exercise, not the main lift
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 2 (1 barbell + 1 event)
- **Intensity range:** Barbell day: 80–90% 1RM (low reps); Event day: 70–80% of implement maximum

---

### Loaded Carry

- **Why this system requires this category:** Strongman is the only strength sport that requires athletes to move under load. Athletes who are strong statically but fail on carry events almost universally lack dynamic trunk rigidity training.
- **What this typically means in this system:** Farmer carry, Yoke walk, Sandbag carry, Husafell stone carry
- **Selection criteria:** Neutral spine maintenance under continuous step rhythm, training the grip and balance characteristics of specific implements
- **The Yoke rule:** Yoke cannot be replaced by a barbell back squat walk — the loading position and stability demands are fundamentally different. If no Yoke is available, a heavy Frame carry is a better substitute than any barbell variation.
- **Grip and straps decision logic:** Grip failure during Farmer carry is one of the most common causes of technical breakdown in intermediate athletes — the muscles still have capacity, but the hands give out first. Apply the following rule consistently:
  - **Barbell strength days (deadlift, row):** Straps are permitted. The goal is posterior chain and strength development; grip is not the limiting factor being trained here, and using straps protects neural capacity for event days.
  - **Event days (Farmer carry, Frame carry):** No straps. Bare grip is mandatory. This is when grip capacity is being trained directly. Accumulating bare-grip volume under implement-specific loading is the only reliable method of building competition-ready grip strength. Straps on event days undermine this adaptation.
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 1 (2 recommended, distributed across both event days)
- **Distance range:** Training: 20–30 m; approaching competition: use full competition course distance

---

### Vertical Pull (Upper Back)

- **Why this system requires this category:** Upper back strength supports trunk stability in all carry events and scapular control in all overhead events. Strongman athletes are systematically weaker in the upper back than the lower back — this becomes a primary injury driver at the advanced level.
- **What this typically means in this system:** Barbell bent-over row (primary), Weighted pull-ups (supplementary)
- **Selection criteria:** High upper back muscle recruitment, supports heavy loading
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 1
- **Intensity range:** 8–12 RM range (low-rep strength work is not required here)

---

### Squat Pattern

- **Why this system requires this category:** The terminal loading position in both Yoke walk and Atlas stone loading places high demands on quad strength at squat depth. The squat is also the most effective general trunk rigidity training tool.
- **What this typically means in this system:** Back squat (primary); Front squat for Atlas stone receiving position training
- **Selection criteria:** Allows wide stance (strongman athletes typically squat wider than powerlifters to match implement demands)
- **Minimum weekly frequency:** 1
- **Intensity range:** 4–6 RM range (1–2 RM intensity is not required)

---

## Milestones

### Technical Indicators

- Complete a 20 m Yoke walk at 1.2× bodyweight with no mid-course step stoppage and no excessive forward lean
- Complete 5 consecutive push press reps on a log at 0.5× bodyweight, with stable receiving position after each rep
- Farmer carry 0.6× bodyweight per hand (combined total 1.2× BW) for 20 m without switching hands, with consistent step rhythm
- Load an 80–100 kg Atlas stone (or equivalent sandbag) to a waist-height platform, rounding the upper back while maintaining lower back tension throughout

### Strength / Fitness Indicators

- Conventional Deadlift ≥ 2.0× bodyweight
- Strict Press ≥ 0.8× bodyweight
- Back Squat ≥ 1.5× bodyweight

**Signal to advance to Advanced level:**
All three strength indicators achieved, at least one formal novice-class competition completed, and all designated competition implements executable to competition standard.

---

## Common Violations

| Violation | Root Cause | Correct Approach |
|-----------|-----------|-----------------|
| Using barbell for all overhead training | Assuming barbell strength transfers completely to log and axle | Event days must use the actual implement; barbell days build the foundation, they do not simulate the competition |
| Scheduling an event day the day after the heaviest barbell day | Course design ignores the non-local nature of neural fatigue | At least one full rest day between the event day and the heaviest barbell day |
| Placing Yoke on Monday (first training day of the week) | Misbelief that the most important exercise should be trained first | Yoke's axial compression demands the most recovered state possible; always place it at the end of the training week |
| Skipping implement training entirely to focus on barbell numbers | Implements are inaccessible, or the athlete over-values quantifiable progress | No implements means no strongman; substitutes (sandbags, dumbbell farmer carries) are mandatory, not optional |
| Attempting heavy implement loads before strength indicators are met | Impatience | Implement technique must be built under controlled loads; learning the wrong motor pattern at excessive weight is a larger setback than the delayed load increase |
| Training more than 5 days per week | Equating training volume with progress | Strongman implement fatigue is largely invisible; 4 days is the upper limit for intermediate athletes; 3 days is appropriate when recovery is suboptimal |
