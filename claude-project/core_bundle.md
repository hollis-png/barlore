# Deload

A deload is a planned period of reduced training stress — typically one week — designed to
allow accumulated fatigue to dissipate so that the fitness developed during the preceding
training block can be fully expressed. Deloads are not rest; they are **strategic reduction
in stimulus** while maintaining enough activity to preserve motor patterns and prevent
physiological detraining.

## Why Deloads Are Necessary

Training accumulates fatigue faster than it accumulates fitness. During a hard training
block, the fatigue layer sits on top of the fitness layer and suppresses performance —
this is why a lifter's 1RM often *increases* after a deload even though no heavy training
occurred in that week. The fitness was there; the fatigue was masking it.

This mechanism follows directly from the SRA curve (see `core/sra_curve.md`): the next
adaptation stimulus can only be fully absorbed once the previous round of fatigue has
cleared. Without a deload, each week's training builds on residual fatigue from the
last, eventually driving the athlete past their Maximum Recoverable Volume (see
`core/volume_landmarks.md`) and into an overreaching or overtraining state.

## Types of Deload

### Volume Deload (most common)
Maintain working intensity (load on bar stays near normal working weights), but reduce
total sets per muscle group by **40–60%**. This keeps the nervous system familiar with
heavy loads while dramatically reducing cumulative fatigue.

*Best for*: strength-focused programs; preserves 1RM readiness.

### Intensity Deload
Maintain training volume (set count), but reduce load to **50–70%** of normal working
weights. Higher rep counts are typical. Joints and connective tissue recover well under
this approach.

*Best for*: hypertrophy phases; cardiovascular and metabolic conditioning is preserved.

### Complete Rest
All training suspended. Rarely optimal beyond 1–2 days; longer periods risk loss of
motor patterns, muscle mass, and psychological disruption. Reserved for acute injury or
illness.

## When to Deload

### Planned Deloads (scheduled)
Insert after a fixed number of hard training weeks — typically every **4–6 weeks** for
intermediate trainees, **3–4 weeks** for advanced trainees. This is the standard approach
in program design (e.g., the fourth week of 5/3/1 is a deload week by design).

Advantages: predictability, easy to track, avoids waiting until fatigue becomes problematic.

### Reactive Deloads (symptom-triggered)
A deload taken in response to observable indicators rather than on a fixed schedule.
Appropriate when self-monitoring is accurate enough to distinguish normal training
fatigue from excessive accumulation.

**Deload indicators**:
- Performance decline across 2+ consecutive sessions on the same lift
- Persistent joint tenderness that does not resolve in 48 hours
- Motivational loss — dread of training rather than normal pre-session inertia
- Sleep quality degradation without other explanation
- DOMS that does not clear between sessions

### In Practice

Most intermediate athletes benefit from planned deloads. Advanced athletes with
well-developed self-monitoring can use a hybrid: schedule a deload window every 4–6 weeks,
but trigger it early if reactive indicators appear sooner.

## How to Structure a Deload Week

**Volume deload template** (recommended for most):

| Session variable | Working week | Deload week |
|-----------------|-------------|------------|
| Sets per muscle group | 100% | 40–60% |
| Load per set | 100% | 90–100% |
| RIR target | 1–3 | 4–6 |
| Session duration | Normal | Shorter |

Keep the exercise selection identical. Maintain frequency if possible — skipping the
gym entirely for a week is not a deload, it is rest.

## What a Deload Is Not

- **Deload ≠ rest week**: complete rest is rarely optimal and is distinct from a deload
- **Deload ≠ weakness**: not deloading is how overtraining occurs; the deload is the
  mechanism that lets accumulated fitness be expressed
- **Deload ≠ always volume reduction**: an intensity deload is equally valid and may be
  preferable in some contexts

## How Systems Differ

Powerlifting programs typically use volume deloads before competition to maintain
bar weight exposure (readiness) while clearing fatigue. 5/3/1 has a built-in deload week
(week 4: 40%–50%–60% sets of 5) every cycle.
Bodybuilding programs may use either type; intensity deloads are common because they
preserve the metabolic and volume adaptations of the preceding block.
Olympic weightlifting uses tapers that are extended deloads — volume drops sharply 1–3
weeks before competition while intensity remains very high, peaking the nervous system.
CrossFit's constantly varied structure provides some built-in recovery; structured deloads
are less formally programmed but still recommended every 4–6 weeks during periods of
structured training.

# Hypertrophy Mechanisms

Skeletal muscle hypertrophy — the increase in muscle fiber cross-sectional area — is the primary structural adaptation to resistance training. Three mechanisms have been proposed to drive this process: mechanical tension, metabolic stress, and muscle damage. Of these, mechanical tension is the primary driver. The other two are contributory but neither necessary nor sufficient on their own.

This distinction matters for programming: it tells you which training variables actually produce growth and which are side effects that trainees sometimes optimize for by mistake.

---

## Mechanical Tension — The Primary Driver

Mechanical tension is the force generated within muscle fibers during active contraction against an external load. It is the single most important stimulus for hypertrophy. When muscle fibers experience sufficient tension for sufficient duration, mechanotransduction pathways (primarily mTOR signaling) are activated, initiating muscle protein synthesis.

Wackerhage et al. (2019) confirmed that mechanotransduction — the process by which muscle cells convert mechanical force into biochemical signals — is the primary pathway linking resistance training to hypertrophic adaptation. The key mechanosensors (titin, costamere proteins, focal adhesion kinase) respond to the magnitude and duration of tension applied to the fiber.

### What Creates Sufficient Tension

Two conditions must be met:

1. **Load magnitude**: The load must be heavy enough to recruit high-threshold motor units (Type II fibers). At loads below ~30% 1RM, high-threshold units are not recruited even to failure. Above ~30% 1RM, sufficient recruitment occurs — but higher loads recruit them earlier in the set, accumulating more effective tension per rep.

2. **Time under load**: The fibers must sustain tension long enough to trigger the signaling cascade. This is why both heavy low-rep sets and moderate-weight high-rep sets produce hypertrophy — they achieve sufficient total tension through different combinations of force and duration.

### Practical Implications

- **Progressive overload is the operational expression of this mechanism.** Adding load, reps, or sets over time increases the total mechanical tension stimulus. See `progressive_overload`.
- **The rep continuum exists because of tension.** The 6–30 rep range produces equivalent hypertrophy when taken near failure (Schoenfeld 2020), because all points in this range generate sufficient mechanical tension — the tradeoff is between per-rep tension (heavy loads) and per-set duration (lighter loads). See `rep_continuum`.
- **Slow eccentrics and tempo training work when they increase time under meaningful tension** — not because "slow" is inherently better, but because they prevent momentum from reducing fiber recruitment.

---

## Metabolic Stress — Contributory, Not Primary

Metabolic stress refers to the accumulation of metabolic byproducts (lactate, hydrogen ions, inorganic phosphate) during resistance exercise, particularly under conditions of sustained muscular contraction that restrict blood flow (occlusion). The "pump" and the burn are sensory markers of metabolic stress.

Schoenfeld (2013) proposed several mechanisms by which metabolic stress may enhance hypertrophy:
- Increased hormonal milieu (acute GH and testosterone elevations)
- Cell swelling (osmotic pressure on the cell membrane may be a hypertrophic signal)
- Reactive oxygen species (ROS) production in moderate amounts may enhance anabolic signaling
- Recruitment of additional motor units as fatigued fibers fail

### What the Evidence Actually Shows

The evidence for metabolic stress as an independent hypertrophic driver is weaker than initially proposed:

- **Acute hormonal elevations** (the post-set GH spike) have been shown to have minimal effect on long-term muscle growth. The systemic hormonal response is too transient and too small to meaningfully alter protein synthesis rates at the muscle level.
- **Cell swelling** has mechanistic plausibility but no direct human RCT evidence isolating it from mechanical tension.
- **Blood-flow restriction (BFR) training** at 20–30% 1RM produces hypertrophy comparable to moderate-load training — this is the strongest evidence for metabolic stress as a contributor, since the loads are too light to generate high mechanical tension through recruitment alone. The occlusion forces early recruitment of high-threshold motor units, confounding the tension vs. stress question.

### Practical Implications

- Metabolic stress is a useful tool, not a training goal. Chasing the pump at the expense of progressive overload is a net negative.
- BFR training is a valid hypertrophy tool when heavy loading is contraindicated (injury, joint pathology, masters athletes).
- High-rep finisher sets and drop sets generate metabolic stress — they may add a small hypertrophic signal on top of the mechanical tension from the working sets, but they should not replace heavy compound work.

---

## Muscle Damage — Likely Not a Driver

Exercise-induced muscle damage (EIMD) was historically proposed as the third hypertrophy mechanism: damage triggers an inflammatory repair cascade that rebuilds the fiber slightly larger than before. This model is intuitively appealing — soreness after training "feels like" growth — but the evidence does not support damage as a necessary or even beneficial stimulus.

Damas et al. (2018) reviewed the evidence and concluded:

1. **Muscle damage and hypertrophy are not correlated.** Eccentric-only protocols produce extreme damage (severe DOMS, CK elevation, force loss for days) but do not produce proportionally more hypertrophy than concentric-only or mixed protocols.
2. **The repeated bout effect eliminates most damage** after the first few sessions of a new stimulus, yet hypertrophy continues for months — the adaptation that matters (protein synthesis) continues long after damage has ceased.
3. **Excessive damage impairs training frequency and volume** — two variables with strong evidence for driving hypertrophy. Damage that prevents the next session within the SRA window is a net negative.

### Practical Implications

- **Soreness is not a proxy for growth.** DOMS indicates unfamiliar eccentric loading, not an optimal hypertrophic stimulus.
- **Novel exercises produce more damage, not more growth.** Constantly varying exercises (a common mistake) maximizes damage and minimizes the progressive overload that actually drives adaptation.
- **Controlled eccentrics are valuable for tension, not for damage.** The hypertrophic benefit of eccentric loading comes from the high force per fiber during lengthening — a mechanical tension effect — not from the microtrauma.

---

## The Hierarchy in Practice

```
Mechanical Tension
  └── THE primary driver. Optimize this through progressive overload.
      Expressed via: load × reps × sets (volume-load), taken near failure.

Metabolic Stress
  └── Contributory. Useful as a secondary tool (BFR, high-rep sets, drop sets).
      Do not optimize at the expense of tension.

Muscle Damage
  └── Side effect, not a stimulus. Minimize where possible.
      It impairs frequency and volume — both of which matter more.
```

The practical takeaway: design programs to maximize mechanical tension accumulation over time (progressive overload + adequate volume + sufficient proximity to failure). Metabolic stress will occur naturally during moderate-to-high rep work. Muscle damage will occur naturally during novel movements and does not need to be sought.

---

## How Systems Differ

- **Bodybuilding**: Explicitly programs for mechanical tension across rep ranges (heavy compounds for tension, isolation for additional volume). Some bodybuilding traditions overvalue metabolic stress ("pump training"), but evidence-based approaches (RP, Helms) prioritize progressive overload on compound movements.
- **Powerlifting**: Maximizes mechanical tension through heavy loading (1–5 reps). Metabolic stress is minimal; muscle damage is managed through conservative volume. Hypertrophy is a secondary goal pursued through supplemental volume work (BBB sets, back-off sets).
- **Olympic Weightlifting**: High per-rep tension but low metabolic stress due to brief sets. Hypertrophy occurs primarily as a side effect of high training frequency and total volume-load, not through dedicated hypertrophy blocks (though some programs include them in off-season).
- **Calisthenics**: Mechanical tension is modulated through leverage changes rather than external load. Metabolic stress is high in high-rep endurance sets. The limited ability to add external load makes progressive overload harder — progression through harder variations is the primary mechanism.
- **CrossFit**: The mixed-modal nature generates high metabolic stress. Mechanical tension varies by workout type (heavy days vs. metcon days). The inconsistent tension stimulus is why CrossFit athletes often supplement with dedicated strength work.
- **Strongman**: High mechanical tension through heavy odd-object work. Metabolic stress is significant during events like medleys and carries. The combination of high tension and metabolic demand is why strongman training is effective for both strength and hypertrophy.

# Periodization

Periodization is the planned manipulation of volume, intensity, and specificity over longer time scales to manage fatigue and peak performance at a target date.

## Core Definition

The body cannot tolerate high intensity and high volume indefinitely. Periodization alternates phases of accumulating and dissipating fatigue so that long-term adaptation continues while overtraining and stagnation are avoided.

## Common Models

- **Linear** — volume decreases and intensity increases over time; the classic pre-competition ramp
- **Undulating** — volume and intensity vary within a week or even a day (DUP)
- **Block** — training is divided into multi-week blocks focused on a single quality (accumulation → transmutation → realization)
- **Conjugate** — multiple qualities are maintained simultaneously, rotating max-effort and dynamic-effort days

## Three Time Scales

| Scale | Duration | Content |
|-------|----------|---------|
| Macrocycle | Months to a year | The full season or training goal |
| Mesocycle | 3–6 weeks | One training block, often ending with a deload |
| Microcycle | About a week | The weekly distribution of training |

## How Systems Differ

Systems favor different models — powerlifting often uses linear or conjugate, bodybuilding often uses undulating or block. Those differences are recorded in each system's `index.md` and `programs/`; this entry defines only the principle itself.

# Progressive Overload

Progressive overload is the systematic increase of training stimulus over time so the
body continually faces demands beyond its current level of adaptation. Without it,
training produces adaptation until the body catches up to the stimulus, then stops.
With it, adaptation continues as long as recovery capacity is not exceeded.

## Core Definition

If the training stimulus stays constant, the body stops producing new structural change
once it has adapted. The essence of progressive overload is keeping adaptation perpetually
a step behind the stimulus — the body chases a target that keeps moving.

This follows directly from the SRA curve: each adaptation cycle raises the baseline
slightly. For the next stimulus to trigger another cycle, it must exceed the new baseline,
not the original one.

## Ways to Apply Overload

Not all overload methods are equivalent. The NSCA identifies load, volume, and frequency
as the primary training variables; the hierarchy of overload methods roughly follows this
order of directness:

| Method | Mechanism | Best applied when |
|--------|-----------|------------------|
| Increase load (weight on bar) | Greater mechanical tension per rep | Primary method for strength; most direct signal to the nervous system |
| Increase total volume (sets × reps) | Greater total mechanical work | Primary method for hypertrophy; accumulates more MPS-stimulating volume |
| Increase frequency | More SRA cycles per week | Useful when per-session volume cannot increase; requires shorter SRA cycles |
| Decrease rest intervals | Increased metabolic stress per unit time | Secondary hypertrophy mechanism; reduces load capacity if overused |
| Improve technical quality | More efficient force production at the same load | Always available; does not require external load increase |
| Increase exercise difficulty or ROM | Greater force demand through more range | Useful in calisthenics and bodyweight training |

## Intensity Zones and Repetition Correspondence

The NSCA provides reference intensity zones that map percentage of 1RM to approximate
repetition maxima. These are population averages, not exact individual predictions:

| % of 1RM | Approximate reps to failure | Primary training quality |
|----------|---------------------------|------------------------|
| 100% | 1 | Maximal strength / 1RM |
| 95% | 2–3 | Near-maximal strength |
| 90% | 4–5 | Strength |
| 85% | 6 | Strength–hypertrophy overlap |
| 80% | 8 | Hypertrophy |
| 75% | 10 | Hypertrophy |
| 70% | 12 | Hypertrophy–endurance overlap |
| 65% | 15 | Muscular endurance |
| 60% | 17–20 | Endurance |

These zones are not strict boundaries. Schoenfeld (2020) synthesizes evidence showing
that hypertrophy occurs across a wide range of loads (roughly 30–85% of 1RM) when sets
are taken close to failure — the proximity to failure, not the exact load, determines
the hypertrophic stimulus. Strength, however, requires heavier loads (≥75% 1RM) to
adequately recruit and stress high-threshold motor units.

## Rate of Progression

Progression rate must match training status:

- **Novice** (< 6 months consistent training): can add load every session. Linear
  progression exploits the high neural and metabolic sensitivity of untrained tissue.
- **Intermediate** (6 months – 2 years): weekly or bi-weekly load increases are
  appropriate. Within-week variation (undulating periodization) sustains progress.
- **Advanced** (> 2 years): monthly load increases are typical. Block periodization
  accumulates volume in one phase and translates it to load in the next.

Adding load faster than the rate of adaptation produces injury, not accelerated gains.

## Overload Without Load Increases

In phases where load cannot increase (recovery week, maintenance phase, injury
management), overload can be maintained through:

- **Volume increase**: add one working set per exercise while holding load constant
- **Technique improvement**: the same load through a deeper or more controlled ROM
  produces greater mechanical work per rep
- **Proximity to failure**: training closer to failure at the same absolute load
  increases the effective stimulus even with no load change

## How Systems Differ

Each system defines "more stimulus" differently. Those differences are recorded in each
system's `index.md`; this entry defines only the principle itself.

- **Powerlifting**: Load progression on the competition lifts is the primary overload
  mechanism. Programs like 5/3/1 use a training max that increases by 5 lbs (upper
  body) or 10 lbs (lower body) per 4-week cycle, ensuring a steady load progression
  over months.
- **Bodybuilding**: Volume progression (adding sets) is the primary mechanism within
  a mesocycle, followed by load progression across mesocycles. Renaissance Periodization
  formalizes this as MEV → MAV → MRV progression within a block.
- **Olympic Weightlifting**: Technical efficiency is a primary overload mechanism —
  the same load lifted with better positioning constitutes a genuine training advance.
  Load progression follows skill mastery, not a fixed schedule.
- **Calisthenics**: Exercise progression (e.g., push-up → archer push-up → one-arm
  push-up) is the primary overload mechanism. Load in the traditional sense is replaced
  by leverage and range of motion demands.

# Rep Continuum

The rep continuum describes how the primary training adaptation — maximal strength,
hypertrophy, or muscular endurance — shifts as repetition ranges and relative load change.
It is the practical translation of the force-time relationship into programming decisions:
**what you are training depends substantially on how many reps you do and how hard each set is.**

## The Traditional Framework

The NSCA's established guidelines provide the foundational model:

| Zone | Rep range | Relative load (% 1RM) | Primary adaptation |
|------|-----------|----------------------|-------------------|
| Maximal strength | 1–5 | ≥85% | Neural — motor unit recruitment, rate coding |
| Hypertrophy | 6–15 | 65–85% | Structural — myofibrillar and sarcoplasmic |
| Muscular endurance | 15+ | <65% | Metabolic — oxidative capacity, lactate tolerance |

These zones are not hard boundaries — they are centers of gravity. A set of 20 reps taken
to near-failure can build meaningful muscle; a set of 3 reps at 90% 1RM also produces
hypertrophy. The zones indicate where each adaptation is *most efficiently* trained, not
where it exclusively occurs.

## What the Meta-Analytic Evidence Shows

A 2017 systematic review and meta-analysis (Schoenfeld, Grgic, Ogborn, Krieger) compared
low-load training (≤60% 1RM, ≥15 reps) against high-load training (≥65% 1RM, ≤15 reps)
for hypertrophy. Key findings:

- **Hypertrophy**: similar outcomes across load ranges when sets are taken to or near failure.
  The "8–12 rep hypertrophy zone" is a practical guideline, not a physiological law.
- **Maximal strength (1RM)**: significantly greater gains in the high-load condition.
  Training heavy is not replaceable for building 1RM strength.
- **Muscular endurance**: greater gains in the low-load condition.
- **Critical caveat**: the proximity-to-failure condition is essential. Low-load sets
  performed far from failure do not produce the same hypertrophy as sets taken near failure
  at any load. Effort is the variable that makes low-load training work for hypertrophy.

## The Mechanisms Behind the Zones

Three mechanisms drive hypertrophy (Schoenfeld, 2010), and rep range influences which
predominates:

1. **Mechanical tension**: the primary driver; high loads create high tension across
   the sarcomere, which is the strongest signal for myofibrillar hypertrophy. Dominant
   in the strength and hypertrophy zones.

2. **Metabolic stress**: accumulation of metabolites (lactate, phosphate, hydrogen ions)
   during high-rep, moderate-load work. Associated with pump and fatigue; contributes to
   hypertrophy through mechanisms that remain partially debated.

3. **Muscle damage**: eccentric phases and lengthened-position loading cause microtrauma.
   Contributes to hypertrophy through the repair and remodeling process. Note: muscle damage
   as an independent mechanism is increasingly questioned in recent literature.

## Practical Implications

**Do not anchor all training to a single rep zone.** A well-rounded program uses all three
zones strategically:

- Heavy work (1–5 reps) builds the neural capacity to express strength and creates the
  loading conditions for high mechanical tension.
- Moderate work (6–15 reps) is the most time-efficient zone for hypertrophy per set.
- High-rep work (15–30 reps) adds volume with lower joint stress and trains the
  metabolic qualities that support repeated-effort performance.

**Proximity to failure matters more than load.** The critical variable for hypertrophy
is not the rep number but whether the working muscle is being recruited maximally near
the end of the set. A set of 30 reps stopped at 20 does not equal a set of 15 taken
to 1 RIR, even if the rep count is higher.

## How Systems Differ

Powerlifting concentrates volume in the 1–5 zone for the competition lifts and uses
moderate zones for accessories. Bodybuilding distributes volume across all zones within
a session but centers most work in the 8–15 range for maximum hypertrophy efficiency.
Olympic weightlifting rarely exceeds 5 reps on the competition lifts due to technical
fatigue; strength accessories use standard zones. Calisthenics operates in high-rep zones
for skill work and cannot directly manipulate load, so progression comes from moving to
harder movement variants rather than changing the rep range.

# RPE and RIR

RPE (Rate of Perceived Exertion) and RIR (Reps in Reserve) are two related tools for
regulating training intensity based on effort rather than a fixed percentage of 1RM.
They are the primary instruments of **autoregulation** — adjusting load on any given day
to reflect actual readiness rather than a pre-planned number that may be too heavy or too
light depending on sleep, stress, and accumulated fatigue.

## Definitions

**RIR — Reps in Reserve**
The number of additional reps you could have completed before reaching technical failure.
RIR 2 means you stopped a set with 2 more reps still available. RIR 0 means the next rep
would have failed.

**RPE — Rate of Perceived Exertion (resistance training scale)**
A 1–10 scale adapted for resistance training by Mike Tuchscherer (Reactive Training Systems).
The direct mapping to RIR:

| RPE | RIR | Description |
|-----|-----|-------------|
| 10  | 0   | Maximal effort — could not do another rep |
| 9   | 1   | One rep remaining |
| 8   | 2   | Two reps remaining — hard set, not a grinder |
| 7   | 3   | Three reps remaining — challenging, still speed in the bar |
| 6   | 4   | Four reps remaining — moderate; bar moves quickly |
| ≤5  | 5+  | Warm-up territory for trained athletes |

The formula: **RPE = 10 − RIR**

## Why RIR Is the More Intuitive Unit

"Two reps left in the tank" is a concrete internal sensation that most trained athletes
can identify. "RPE 8" requires translating a number back into that sensation. Both convey
the same information; RIR is typically easier to communicate and teach, especially for
athletes unfamiliar with Tuchscherer's original system.

A 2016 RCT (Zourdos et al.) validated the RIR-based RPE scale against actual performance
in trained athletes. Intraclass correlation was high in trained subjects — meaning the
self-reported RIR closely matched true remaining capacity. Accuracy was substantially lower
in untrained individuals and in very high rep ranges (15+), where effort perception
becomes less reliable.

## What Autoregulation Solves

Fixed percentage programming assumes your 1RM is stable day-to-day. It is not. Strength
can vary 5–10% between a well-recovered and a fatigued session. If your program prescribes
3×5 at 85% 1RM on a day when your real 1RM is running 8% lower than tested, you are
actually training at ~93% — far harder than intended, accumulating excess fatigue.

RPE/RIR-based programming avoids this by anchoring to effort:
- "Work up to a set of 5 at RPE 8" means the same relative difficulty regardless of the
  day's absolute strength level.
- The load adjusts automatically to the athlete's actual readiness.

## When to Use RPE vs. Fixed Percentages

Both have a place:

| Scenario | Recommended approach |
|----------|---------------------|
| Peaking / competition prep | Fixed percentages (predictability is priority) |
| Volume accumulation blocks | RPE/RIR (daily readiness varies; effort target is stable) |
| Beginners | Fixed percentages (RPE accuracy requires training history to calibrate) |
| Advanced athletes | RPE/RIR or a hybrid (percentages set the target; RPE guides daily adjustment) |
| Programs like 5/3/1 | Hybrid — percentages for main sets, AMRAP provides RIR data |

## How Systems Differ

Powerlifting programs with a competition date use percentages in the peaking block for
predictability. Volume phases increasingly use RPE to manage fatigue without testing a
true 1RM every cycle. Bodybuilding programs use RIR almost exclusively — proximity to
failure (0–3 RIR) is the key hypertrophy stimulus, and the absolute load is secondary.
Olympic weightlifting rarely uses RPE because technical failure (position breakdown) occurs
before muscular failure, making RPE a poor proxy for actual training stress in that domain.

# Specificity

Specificity is the principle that **the body adapts specifically to the type of stress
placed upon it.** Adaptations are not general: they are tuned to the exact mechanical,
metabolic, and neural demands of the training stimulus. This is formalized as the
**SAID principle** — Specific Adaptation to Imposed Demands.

## Core Definition

A training adaptation transfers most fully to tasks that closely resemble the training
exercise in: movement pattern, load, velocity, and energy system demands. The further a
target task departs from the training stimulus, the less adaptation transfers.

This has a practical corollary: **you become best at what you train most directly.**
A powerlifter who only trains the back squat will outperform on the back squat compared
to a front squat. A sprinter who only trains 40-meter sprints will not be as fit for
a 400-meter race as their sprint performance might suggest.

## The Four Dimensions of Specificity

### 1. Movement Pattern
Adaptations are joint-angle and muscle-group specific. Strength gained at one joint angle
does not transfer completely to a different angle — this is why competition powerlifters
train to depth (hip crease below knee), not above parallel, and why the bottom position
of the squat must be specifically trained.

### 2. Load and Force Requirements
Maximal strength training (heavy loads, low reps) does not produce the same endurance
adaptations as high-rep training, and vice versa. Adaptations to heavy work — increased
motor unit recruitment, improved rate coding, tendon stiffness — are distinct from
adaptations to metabolic work.

### 3. Velocity
The force-velocity curve means that power trained at high velocity transfers poorly to
slow, heavy grinding strength, and slow-speed strength training transfers poorly to fast
explosive performance. Olympic weightlifters must train at high velocity; powerlifters can
benefit from both slow and moderate-velocity work in their phases.

### 4. Energy System
Aerobic training adapts aerobic pathways (mitochondrial density, oxidative enzyme
activity, stroke volume). Anaerobic training adapts glycolytic and phosphocreatine
pathways. Cross-adaptation occurs but is partial: an aerobically-trained athlete has
better oxidative support for strength training than an untrained person, but this does
not substitute for strength-specific work.

## Transfer of Training

Not all exercises transfer equally to the target performance. Transfer is maximized when
training exercises share the following with the target task (Siff & Verkhoshansky):

- The same muscles acting in the same sequence
- The same joint angles at peak force production
- The same velocity profile through the range of motion

This is why **competition-specific practice** is irreplaceable for advanced athletes.
Foundational movements (barbell squat, deadlift, bench press) transfer broadly within
their pattern families but cannot substitute for the specific skill being developed.

## Specificity vs. Variation

Specificity is not a mandate for doing only one exercise. General strength provides the
physical foundation that specific training refines. The training year is typically
organized so that general strength work dominates early phases and specificity increases
as competition approaches. This is the underlying rationale for periodization.

A beginner benefits from general training because they have no adaptive ceiling in any
specific pattern. An advanced athlete is limited by specificity — their general strength
may be adequate, but their ability to express it in the competition movement may not be.
Specificity becomes the limiter as training age increases.

## How Systems Differ

Powerlifting maximizes specificity to three fixed movements and trains them nearly year-round.
Bodybuilding applies specificity to muscle groups rather than movements — the goal is to
stimulate a specific muscle regardless of which exercise achieves it.
Olympic weightlifting demands the highest specificity — the snatch and clean & jerk are
so technically complex that substitutes do not exist; only the competition lifts (and their
direct derivatives) develop the required skill.
CrossFit explicitly de-emphasizes specificity in favor of breadth — the goal is GPP
(General Physical Preparedness) across all domains.
Strongman occupies a middle position: competition events vary, so training must cover
broad patterns while still specifically practicing upcoming event implements.

# SRA Curve

The SRA (Stimulus–Recovery–Adaptation) curve describes the fatigue, recovery, and
supercompensation a body goes through after a single training stimulus. It is the
mechanistic foundation of why all periodized programs work — and why programs that
ignore recovery timing produce stagnation or overtraining.

## Historical Context: GAS vs. SRA

Early periodization theory borrowed Hans Selye's **General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS)**
as its conceptual foundation. Selye's original work, however, was based on systemic,
lethal-level pharmacological stressors — not the localized, sub-maximal mechanical
stress of resistance training. Buckner et al. (2017) demonstrated that GAS models
systemic non-specific responses poorly suited to the localized tissue microtrauma and
neuromuscular adaptations that resistance training induces.

The SRA framework (derived from GAS but applied correctly) addresses this by modeling
**localized depletion and supercompensation** of specific tissues — muscle fiber proteins,
glycogen stores, connective tissue — rather than a systemic alarm response. Cunanan et al.
(2018) formalized the connection: the Alarm, Resistance, and Exhaustion stages of GAS
correspond broadly to the Stimulus, Recovery, and (if training load exceeds recoverable
capacity) Overtraining phases of SRA — but the practical application requires treating
each muscle group's SRA curve as independent.

## Core Definition

Training lowers functional performance in the moment (fatigue phase), which then rebounds
during recovery and, in the supercompensation phase, rises above the original baseline.
The best time for the next session falls at the **peak of supercompensation** — not while
fatigue is still present, and not after supercompensation has decayed back to baseline.

Miss the window too early: training on residual fatigue accumulates damage faster than
adaptation. Miss it too late: the supercompensation decays and each session returns to
baseline rather than building on a higher floor.

## Three Phases

1. **Stimulus** — training causes muscular microtrauma and metabolic stress; performance
   temporarily drops below baseline; the alarm phase in GAS terms
2. **Recovery** — nutrition and sleep drive repair; inflammatory resolution clears the
   acute damage signal; performance rebounds toward baseline
3. **Adaptation** — supercompensation sets a new baseline slightly above the previous
   one; this is when the fitness gain is expressed

A fourth state — **exhaustion** (GAS) / **non-functional overreaching** or **overtraining**
(sports science) — occurs when repeated stimuli are applied without adequate recovery,
driving performance below baseline for weeks or months.

## Curve Length and Training Volume

The SRA cycle length is determined by the magnitude of the stimulus:

| Stimulus magnitude | Approximate SRA cycle | Example |
|-------------------|----------------------|---------|
| Low (single small-muscle isolation) | 24–36 h | Cable curl, lateral raise |
| Moderate (compound moderate volume) | 48–72 h | Moderate-volume squat session |
| High (high-volume large-muscle compound) | 72–96 h or more | Heavy high-volume deadlift session |

These ranges are approximate and vary significantly by training status. Novices have
longer SRA cycles because their damage response is proportionally greater relative to
their recovery capacity. Advanced athletes have shorter cycles because their recovery
infrastructure (hormonal environment, local tissue resilience) is more developed.

This directly determines **training frequency design**: a muscle group trained with high
volume requires more days before the next stimulus than one trained with low volume.
Frequency and volume are co-determined by the SRA curve, not independently set.

## Interaction with Volume Landmarks

Volume accumulation across a mesocycle gradually extends the SRA curve because cumulative
fatigue adds to the acute fatigue of each session. This is why performance often declines
in the final weeks of a hard training block even when per-session stimulus is unchanged —
the accumulated fatigue layer lengthens effective SRA cycle duration beyond the available
recovery window. A deload resets this accumulated fatigue, allowing supercompensation to
be expressed.

## How Systems Differ

Each system arranges different stimulus density and recovery intervals to suit its goals.
Those differences are recorded in each system's `index.md`; this entry defines only the
principle itself.

- **Powerlifting**: Frequency is calibrated so that the next heavy session falls at or
  near supercompensation peak for the competition lifts. This typically means 2–3x/week
  per lift at submaximal loads, with the highest-intensity session occurring when fatigue
  from prior sessions has fully cleared.
- **Bodybuilding**: Higher volume per session extends SRA cycles. Training frequency is
  typically 1–2x/week per muscle group, with the second session placed when soreness and
  performance have returned to baseline.
- **Olympic Weightlifting**: Daily or twice-daily training is possible because session
  volumes are often low per movement; the SRA curve for a single lift at low volume
  resolves in 24 hours, allowing high frequency.
- **CrossFit**: The mixed-modal structure makes SRA modeling complex; glycogen SRA and
  muscular SRA operate on different timescales. A 48-hour recovery window between
  high-intensity sessions is the minimum practical rule.

# Testing Protocols

Most training programs prescribe intensity as a percentage of a reference number — either a true 1RM (one-repetition maximum) or a training max. Knowing how to establish, estimate, and re-test these numbers is a prerequisite for following any percentage-based program. This entry covers when and how to test, how to estimate without testing, and how to set a training max.

---

## True 1RM Testing

A true 1RM test determines the maximum weight you can lift for a single repetition with correct technique. It is the most accurate way to calibrate program intensities, but it carries trade-offs: it requires experience with heavy singles, generates significant fatigue, and carries elevated injury risk for beginners.

### When to Test

- **Before starting a percentage-based program** that uses `pct_1rm` intensity (e.g., Texas Method, Catalyst 12-Week).
- **At the end of a training block** to measure progress — typically after a deload week, when fatigue is cleared.
- **In competition** (powerlifting, Olympic weightlifting, strongman).

### When Not to Test

- **Beginners (< 6 months training)**: Technique under maximal load is unreliable. Use estimated 1RM or training max instead.
- **Mid-block**: Testing during an accumulation phase wastes a session and produces artificially low numbers due to accumulated fatigue.
- **Frequent retesting**: 1RM testing more often than every 8–12 weeks is unnecessary. Strength changes slowly enough that monthly testing produces noise, not signal.

### Protocol (NSCA Standard)

The NSCA protocol (Haff & Triplett, 2016) is the gold standard for 1RM testing:

1. **General warm-up**: 5–10 min light cardio.
2. **Specific warm-up ramp**:
   - 50% estimated 1RM × 8 reps
   - 70% × 5 reps
   - 80% × 3 reps
   - 90% × 1 rep
3. **Testing attempts**:
   - Attempt 1: 95% estimated 1RM (should feel heavy but confident)
   - Attempt 2: +2.5–5 kg if Attempt 1 succeeded; repeat previous weight if failed
   - Attempt 3: +2.5 kg if Attempt 2 succeeded; stop if failed
   - Maximum 3–5 attempts. Rest 3–5 minutes between attempts.
4. **Record the highest successful single** as the 1RM.

**Critical rules**:
- The rep must meet the movement standard: full depth on squats, pause on bench (if testing for powerlifting), full lockout on deadlifts.
- If form breaks down significantly, the attempt does not count even if completed.
- Always use a spotter for squat and bench press; use safety bars or pins if training alone.

---

## Estimated 1RM (Rep-Max Formulas)

When a true 1RM test is impractical, you can estimate it from a submaximal set. Several formulas exist; the two most widely used:

### Epley Formula

```
Estimated 1RM = weight × (1 + reps / 30)
```

### Brzycki Formula

```
Estimated 1RM = weight × (36 / (37 − reps))
```

Both formulas are most accurate in the 3–10 rep range (LeSuer et al. 1997). Above 10 reps, they increasingly overestimate; below 3, they approach the true 1RM anyway.

### Example

You bench press 80 kg for 6 reps:
- Epley: 80 × (1 + 6/30) = 80 × 1.2 = **96 kg**
- Brzycki: 80 × (36 / 31) = 80 × 1.161 = **93 kg**

The true value is likely between the two estimates. For programming purposes, use the **lower estimate** — an underestimated 1RM produces slightly lighter working weights, which is safer than the reverse.

### When to Use Estimates

- **Beginners**: Always. Run a "top set" at RPE 8–9 for 3–5 reps and plug into the formula.
- **Program start**: Many programs don't require a true 1RM — an estimate from a 3–5RM test is sufficient.
- **Progress tracking**: Re-estimate every 4–6 weeks using a heavy set within normal training. No separate test session needed.

---

## Training Max (TM)

The training max is a deliberately conservative number used to calculate working weights. It ensures that prescribed percentages remain submaximal even on bad days, preserving technique quality and sustainable progression.

### What It Is

A training max is typically set at **85–90% of the true or estimated 1RM**. Wendler (2011) popularized this concept in the 5/3/1 program, recommending 90% for experienced lifters and 85% for beginners or when starting a new cycle.

```
Training Max = Estimated 1RM × 0.85 (conservative) or × 0.90 (standard)
```

### Why It Exists

- **Accounts for daily variation**: Strength fluctuates ±5% based on sleep, stress, nutrition, and accumulated fatigue. A TM at 90% ensures that even a "bad day" 1RM is above the TM.
- **Preserves rep quality**: Working sets calculated from a TM are executable with good form for the prescribed reps. Sets calculated from a true 1RM often require grinding reps that degrade technique.
- **Enables long-term progression**: Programs like 5/3/1 add 2.5–5 kg to the TM per cycle. Starting at 90% gives 6–12 months of progression before the TM approaches the true 1RM.

### Example

| True 1RM | TM (90%) | Week 1 working weight (5/3/1: 65% TM) | Effective % of true 1RM |
|----------|----------|----------------------------------------|------------------------|
| 140 kg | 126 kg | 82 kg | 59% |
| 140 kg | 119 kg (85%) | 77 kg | 55% |

The gap between TM-based percentages and true 1RM percentages is the built-in safety margin.

### Programs That Use TM

| Intensity notation | Meaning | Example programs |
|-------------------|---------|-----------------|
| `pct_tm` | Percentage of training max | 5/3/1, 5/3/1 BBB |
| `pct_1rm` | Percentage of true 1RM | Texas Method, most Olympic programs |
| `rpe` | Autoregulated, no fixed % | RP Hypertrophy, GZCLP (tier system) |

---

## Re-Testing and TM Updates

### When to Re-Test

| Scenario | Action |
|----------|--------|
| End of a 4-week 5/3/1 cycle | Add 2.5 kg (upper) or 5 kg (lower) to TM. No re-test needed. |
| End of a 12-week block | Optional 1RM test after deload week |
| Program switch | Estimate from a recent heavy set; start the new program conservatively |
| Return from injury or layoff | Re-test from scratch; do not use old numbers |
| Stall (2+ cycles without AMRAP improvement) | Reset TM to 85–90% of current estimated 1RM |

### How to Re-Test Without a Max-Out Session

Many programs build testing into normal training:

- **AMRAP sets** (5/3/1, GZCLP): The rep count on the AMRAP set provides an ongoing estimated 1RM. If your AMRAP consistently exceeds the prescribed minimum, your estimated 1RM has increased. No separate test needed.
- **RPE-based top sets**: If you hit a prescribed RPE 8 single, that single is approximately your 92% 1RM (see `rpe_rir`). Multiply by 1.08 to estimate current 1RM.
- **Rep PR tracking**: If you beat a previous rep record at the same weight, your 1RM has increased even without testing it directly.

---

## Testing for Non-Barbell Systems

### Calisthenics

There is no "1RM" for bodyweight exercises. Instead, test:
- **Max reps**: Unbroken set to failure (pull-ups, push-ups, dips)
- **Max hold time**: Handstand, L-sit, planche (seconds)
- **Progression level**: Which variation can be performed for the target rep range (e.g., tuck front lever for 10s but not advanced tuck)

Re-test every 4–6 weeks. Progress is expressed as either more reps, longer holds, or advancing to a harder variation.

### CrossFit

CrossFit uses benchmark WODs (Fran, Grace, Murph) as testing proxies. Barbell lifts are tested as true 1RMs in strength cycles. Gymnastic movements use max reps or max unbroken sets.

### Strongman

Event-specific testing: max deadlift, max log press, max distance carry at fixed weight, fastest time for a medley. These are tested in mock competitions or at the end of training blocks.

---

## Common Mistakes

1. **Testing too often**: Monthly 1RM tests are unnecessary fatigue generators. Every 8–12 weeks is sufficient.
2. **Testing when fatigued**: Always test after a deload or light week. A 1RM test during Week 4 of a volume block will underestimate your true max.
3. **Using true 1RM for programming**: Unless a program explicitly calls for `pct_1rm`, use a training max. Programming off a true 1RM produces overly heavy working sets that degrade form and limit volume.
4. **Skipping warm-up sets**: The ramp protocol is not optional. Cold muscles produce lower maxes and higher injury risk.
5. **Ego-driven attempts**: Stop at 3 attempts if progress has stalled. A 4th or 5th grinding attempt produces excessive fatigue and injury risk for diminishing information.

---

## How Systems Differ

- **Powerlifting**: The most formalized testing culture. Competition is itself a 1RM test. Training cycles peak toward a test day with planned deloads. Most programs use either `pct_1rm` or `pct_tm`.
- **Bodybuilding**: 1RM testing is uncommon. Progress is tracked through rep PRs, visual progress, and estimated 1RM from working sets. Programs use `rpe` or rep-range targets rather than percentages.
- **Olympic Weightlifting**: Regular 1RM testing on snatch and clean & jerk is standard because competition is at 1RM. Daily training often includes heavy singles as autoregulation. Training percentages are based on recent best, updated frequently.
- **Calisthenics**: No barbell 1RM. Progress is tracked through rep maxes and progression advancement. See the calisthenics section above.
- **CrossFit**: Periodic 1RM testing for benchmark lifts (back squat, deadlift, clean, snatch). WOD benchmarks serve as fitness tests.
- **Strongman**: Event-specific testing in mock competitions. Barbell lifts are tested similarly to powerlifting.

# Training to Failure

Training to failure means performing repetitions until the concentric phase of the movement cannot be completed with acceptable form — the point at which the working muscles can no longer generate sufficient force to move the load through the full range of motion. This is distinct from technical failure (form breaks down), cardiovascular failure (you run out of breath), or psychological failure (you stop because it hurts).

Whether to train to failure, how often, and on which exercises is one of the most consequential programming decisions — more so than most trainees realize. The answer is not binary. Failure is a tool with specific applications, costs, and diminishing returns.

---

## What the Evidence Shows

### For Hypertrophy

Grgic et al. (2022, meta-analysis) found that **training to failure produces a small but statistically significant advantage for hypertrophy compared to stopping 1–3 reps short**, when total volume is equated. However, the effect size is modest, and the practical significance depends on context:

- The advantage is most pronounced on **isolation exercises** and single-joint movements, where failure does not create disproportionate systemic fatigue.
- On **compound exercises** (squat, deadlift, bench press), training to failure generates substantially more fatigue per set, reducing the total number of quality sets that can be performed in the session and across the week. The net effect on weekly volume — the stronger hypertrophy driver — can be negative.

**Key finding**: Proximity to failure matters more than reaching failure itself. Sets stopped at RPE 8–9 (1–2 RIR) capture the vast majority of the hypertrophic stimulus while preserving the ability to perform more total volume.

### For Strength

Martorelli et al. (2017, RCT) demonstrated that training to failure does not produce superior maximal strength gains compared to stopping 2–3 reps short, when intensity (% 1RM) is matched. For strength development, the primary drivers are load intensity and movement-specific practice — not proximity to failure.

Training to failure on heavy compound lifts (>85% 1RM) carries additional risks:
- Technique degradation under maximal fatigue, which reinforces poor motor patterns
- Elevated injury risk due to compensatory movement strategies
- Extended SRA cycles that reduce training frequency

---

## The Cost of Failure

Every set taken to failure costs more recovery than a set stopped short. This cost is disproportionate — the last 1–2 reps of a failure set generate a fatigue signal that exceeds their contribution to mechanical tension.

| Sets to failure per session | Recovery cost | Practical impact |
|----------------------------|---------------|------------------|
| 0 (all sets RPE 7–8) | Low | Maximum volume tolerance; suitable for high-frequency programs |
| 1–2 (last set of each exercise) | Moderate | Good balance of stimulus and recovery; most evidence-based programs use this |
| All sets | High | Significantly reduces weekly volume capacity; appropriate only for low-volume, low-frequency programs |

Israetel (2019) describes this as the **"stimulus-to-fatigue ratio" (SFR)**: failure maximizes per-set stimulus but also maximizes per-set fatigue. The optimal programming strategy maximizes total weekly stimulus, not per-set stimulus — which means most sets should stop 1–3 reps short of failure.

---

## When to Train to Failure

### Good Applications

1. **Last set of an isolation exercise** — Cable curls, lateral raises, leg extensions. The fatigue cost is localized and does not compromise subsequent exercises. This is where failure adds the most hypertrophic value per unit of fatigue.

2. **AMRAP sets in structured programs** — Programs like 5/3/1 use a single AMRAP (As Many Reps As Possible) set as a built-in autoregulation tool. The set goes to or near failure, and the rep count provides data for progression decisions.

3. **Deload-preceding sessions** — The final session before a planned deload can include more failure training, since the deload provides the extended recovery window.

4. **Intensity techniques (drop sets, rest-pause, myo-reps)** — These methods deliberately use failure as a mechanism to extend a set beyond the initial failure point. They are most effective on isolation movements and during dedicated hypertrophy blocks.

5. **Machine and cable exercises** — Failure on machines is safer than free weights because the movement path is constrained and there is no balance component. The fatigue cost is lower relative to the stimulus because stabilizer demand is reduced.

### Poor Applications

1. **Heavy compound movements (squat, deadlift, overhead press)** — The fatigue cost is systemic, the injury risk at failure is meaningful, and technique degradation under maximal fatigue builds bad motor patterns.

2. **Every set of every exercise** — This approach maximizes per-session fatigue, reduces total weekly volume, and leads to plateau or regression within 3–4 weeks without a deload.

3. **Beginners** — Novice lifters cannot accurately gauge proximity to failure. They tend to either stop too far from failure (reducing the stimulus) or push through dangerous form breakdown. Beginners should train at RPE 6–7 (3–4 RIR) while they develop the body awareness to calibrate effort. See `rpe_rir`.

4. **Early sets in a session** — Taking the first set to failure compromises all subsequent sets of that exercise. If you plan 4 sets, only the 4th should approach failure.

---

## Failure and RPE/RIR

Training to failure is RPE 10 / RIR 0. The RPE/RIR system exists precisely to calibrate proximity to failure without actually reaching it. See `rpe_rir` for the full scale.

The evidence-supported sweet spot for most hypertrophy training:

| Population | Recommended proximity | RPE | RIR |
|------------|----------------------|-----|-----|
| Beginner | Moderate | 6–7 | 3–4 |
| Intermediate | Close | 7–9 | 1–3 |
| Advanced | Very close, selectively to failure | 8–10 | 0–2 |

As training age increases, the ability to accurately self-regulate improves, and the need for high proximity to failure increases (because the adaptation threshold rises). Advanced trainees need to train closer to failure to continue progressing, but even they should reserve true failure for select sets and exercises.

---

## Failure in Periodization Context

Within a mesocycle (typically 4–6 weeks), proximity to failure typically increases as the block progresses:

- **Week 1**: RPE 7–8 on most sets. Establishing working weights, building volume.
- **Weeks 2–3**: RPE 8–9. Volume accumulation phase. Some final sets approach failure.
- **Week 4**: RPE 9–10 on select sets. Peak stimulus before deload. More sets at or near failure.
- **Deload**: RPE 5–6. Recovery week. No sets near failure.

This structure — sometimes called a **"ramp to failure"** — allows progressive stimulus escalation while managing cumulative fatigue. The deload resets the accumulated fatigue, and the next mesocycle begins the ramp again from a higher baseline.

---

## How Systems Differ

- **Bodybuilding**: The most failure-tolerant system. Isolation exercises are regularly taken to failure; compound lifts typically stop 1–2 reps short. Intensity techniques (drop sets, rest-pause, supersets to failure) are standard tools, especially in hypertrophy blocks. The RP system explicitly programs failure on the last set of the last exercise for each muscle group.
- **Powerlifting**: Failure is almost never programmed on competition lifts. Heavy singles, doubles, and triples stop at RPE 8–9 (1–2 reps in reserve). Technique quality under fatigue degrades the movement pattern that must be preserved for competition. Accessory work may approach failure.
- **Olympic Weightlifting**: Training to failure is avoided entirely on the competition lifts (snatch, clean & jerk). These are technically demanding ballistic movements where failure means a missed lift — the consequences are a dropped barbell, not a failed rep. Squats and pulls may be pushed harder but still rarely to true failure.
- **Calisthenics**: Failure occurs naturally on bodyweight movements when the trainee cannot complete another rep. Since load cannot be reduced mid-set (unlike a barbell), failure is more common and less avoidable. Programs typically manage this by prescribing sets well below max reps (e.g., 3×5 when max is 8).
- **CrossFit**: Failure within a metcon is managed by pacing and scaling, not by programming to failure. Dedicated strength work follows the same failure guidelines as powerlifting: stop 1–2 reps short on compounds.
- **Strongman**: Heavy event training (max deadlift, log press attempts) inherently approaches failure. Assistance work is managed similarly to bodybuilding — isolation exercises may go to failure, compounds stop short.

# Volume Landmarks

Volume landmarks are a framework for quantifying the training volume — measured in weekly
sets per muscle group — at which distinct adaptations occur. They answer the question:
**how much is enough, how much is optimal, and how much is too much?**

## The Four Landmarks

**MV — Maintenance Volume**
The minimum weekly sets required to preserve existing muscle mass. No further growth
occurs at this volume, but current size is retained. Relevant during peaking phases or
competition prep, when fatigue management takes priority over adding stimulus.

**MEV — Minimum Effective Volume**
The least volume per week that produces measurable progress for a given muscle group.
Below MEV, you maintain but do not grow. MEV is the starting point for a hypertrophy
mesocycle — week one sets begin here. It is lower than most trainees assume: for some
muscle groups, 6–8 sets per week may be sufficient to initiate growth in intermediate
lifters.

**MAV — Maximum Adaptive Volume**
The volume range within which the greatest hypertrophic adaptation occurs. MAV is a range
rather than a fixed number, and it shifts upward as training age and work capacity increase.
The goal of a well-designed mesocycle is to begin near MEV and progressively increase volume
across weeks until approaching MAV — adding stimulus while fatigue is still manageable.

**MRV — Maximum Recoverable Volume**
The highest weekly volume from which the body can still recover before the next session.
Exceeding MRV produces fatigue accumulation that outpaces adaptation: performance declines,
injury risk rises, and further volume becomes counterproductive. MRV is the ceiling of a
mesocycle; when approaching it, a deload is due.

## Landmark Order

```
MV  <  MEV  <  MAV  <  MRV
```

## Why Volume Matters: The Dose-Response Evidence

A 2017 meta-analysis (Schoenfeld, Ogborn, Krieger) confirmed a dose-response relationship
between weekly training volume and hypertrophy: more sets per muscle group produce more
growth, up to a recoverable ceiling. The practical takeaway is that most trainees
underperform because they train below MEV rather than because they need more advanced
techniques.

## Practical Application

A typical hypertrophy mesocycle runs 4–6 weeks:
- **Week 1**: volume at or near MEV. Sessions feel manageable.
- **Weeks 2–4**: volume increases by 1–2 sets per muscle group per week.
- **Final week**: volume approaching MRV. Sessions feel hard; fatigue accumulates.
- **Deload week**: volume drops to MV or below. Fatigue clears, fitness is expressed.

## How Systems Differ

The landmark framework applies universally, but the absolute numbers shift significantly
by goal. Powerlifting programs keep volume for non-competition movements near MEV to
preserve recovery capacity for the competition lifts. Bodybuilding programs push toward
MAV for the target muscle groups each mesocycle. Strength athletes often operate with
lower overall set counts but higher average intensity, which changes recovery demands
and therefore MRV.

## What This Framework Does Not Replace

Volume landmarks describe weekly set volume. They do not capture:
- Load intensity (percentage of 1RM or RPE)
- Proximity to failure (sets done far from failure accumulate less stimulus at the same
  set count)
- Exercise selection quality (a poorly targeted exercise contributes fewer effective sets)

See `core/rpe_rir.md` for intensity regulation and `core/sra_curve.md` for the
recovery mechanism underlying the MRV ceiling.
