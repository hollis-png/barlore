## Sources

### Source 1 (primary)
- Title: "Strength and Hypertrophy Adaptations Between Low- vs. High-Load Resistance
  Training: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis"
- Author(s): Schoenfeld, Grgic, Ogborn, Krieger
- Year: 2017
- Journal: Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research
- DOI: 10.1519/JSC.0000000000002200
- Credibility: meta_analysis
- Key facts extracted:
  - Both low-load (≤60% 1RM, ~15+ reps) and high-load (≥65% 1RM, ≤15 reps) training
    produce similar hypertrophy when sets are taken to or near failure
  - High-load training produces significantly greater strength gains (1RM)
  - Low-load training produces greater local muscular endurance gains
  - The "hypertrophy rep range" (8–12 reps) is a practical guideline, not a strict boundary;
    meaningful hypertrophy occurs across a broad rep range
  - Volume (sets × reps × load) equated → adaptations converge for hypertrophy
- Limitations / caveats:
  - Most studies short-term (8–16 weeks); long-term adaptations less clear
  - "Near failure" is critical — low-load sets not taken near failure show less hypertrophy
  - Trained vs. untrained populations may respond differently

### Source 2
- Title: "NSCA's Essentials of Strength Training and Conditioning" (4th ed.)
- Author(s): Haff, Triplett (eds.)
- Year: 2016
- Publisher: Human Kinetics / NSCA
- Credibility: expert_consensus
- Key facts extracted:
  - Traditional rep range guidelines:
    - 1–5 reps @ ≥85% 1RM → maximal strength / neural adaptations
    - 6–12 reps @ 67–85% 1RM → hypertrophy (primary zone)
    - 12–20+ reps @ <67% 1RM → muscular endurance
  - These are starting points for program design; not hard limits
  - Neural adaptations dominate early; structural (hypertrophy) adaptations increase
    with sustained training over months
- Limitations / caveats:
  - Guidelines predate the meta-analytic evidence showing broader hypertrophy range

### Source 3
- Title: "The Mechanisms of Muscle Hypertrophy and Their Application to Resistance Training"
- Author(s): Schoenfeld
- Year: 2010
- Journal: Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research
- DOI: 10.1519/JSC.0b013e3181e840f3
- Credibility: expert_consensus (review paper)
- Key facts extracted:
  - Three proposed mechanisms of hypertrophy: mechanical tension, metabolic stress,
    muscle damage
  - High loads primarily drive mechanical tension
  - Higher rep / lower load work drives metabolic stress
  - Both pathways can produce hypertrophy; the rep range determines which mechanism
    is the primary driver
- Limitations / caveats:
  - Mechanistic review; mechanisms are still actively researched and debated
  - Muscle damage as a driver is increasingly questioned in recent literature
