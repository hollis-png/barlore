## Sources

### Source 1
- Title: "NSCA's Essentials of Strength Training and Conditioning" (4th ed.)
- Author(s): Haff, Triplett (eds.)
- Year: 2016
- Publisher: Human Kinetics / NSCA
- Credibility: expert_consensus
- Key facts extracted:
  - SAID = Specific Adaptation to Imposed Demands
  - The body adapts specifically to the stress placed upon it; adaptations are not general
  - Specificity dimensions:
    1. Movement pattern: adaptations transfer most to movements that closely resemble
       the training exercise (joint angles, ROM, muscle groups)
    2. Load / force: strength at heavy loads does not transfer fully to endurance tasks
       and vice versa
    3. Velocity: power trained at high velocity transfers poorly to slow heavy strength,
       and vice versa (force-velocity curve specificity)
    4. Energy system: aerobic training adapts aerobic pathways; anaerobic training adapts
       anaerobic pathways; cross-adaptation is partial and limited
  - Practical implication: the closer training resembles the target performance, the
    greater the transfer; foundational movements (squat, deadlift) transfer broadly within
    their pattern but not across unrelated patterns
- Limitations / caveats:
  - Specificity is a principle, not an absolute; some cross-transfer always occurs
  - For beginners, general strength training transfers broadly; for advanced athletes,
    specificity becomes the limiting factor

### Source 2
- Title: "Transfer of Training" (chapter in Supertraining)
- Author(s): Siff, Verkhoshansky
- Year: 2003 (6th ed.)
- Publisher: Supertraining Institute
- Credibility: expert_consensus
- Key facts extracted:
  - "Transfer of training" is maximized when training exercise and target task share:
    - Same muscles in the same sequence
    - Same joint angles at peak force
    - Same velocity profile
  - Conjugate periodization explicitly attempts to combine high-specificity and
    high-general-strength work to maximize transfer
- Limitations / caveats:
  - Verkhoshansky's work is influential but predates modern RCT methodology
  - Some claims extrapolated from Eastern Bloc sports science; not all independently
    replicated
