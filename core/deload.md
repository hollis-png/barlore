# Deload

A deload is a planned period of reduced training stress — typically one week — designed to
allow accumulated fatigue to dissipate so that the fitness developed during the preceding
training block can be fully expressed. Deloads are not rest; they are **strategic reduction
in stimulus** while maintaining enough activity to preserve motor patterns and prevent
physiological detraining.

## Why Deloads Are Necessary

Training accumulates fatigue faster than it accumulates fitness. During a hard training
block, the fatigue layer sits on top of the fitness layer and suppresses performance —
this is why a lifter's 1RM often *increases* after a deload even though no heavy training
occurred in that week. The fitness was there; the fatigue was masking it.

This mechanism follows directly from the SRA curve (see `core/sra_curve.md`): the next
adaptation stimulus can only be fully absorbed once the previous round of fatigue has
cleared. Without a deload, each week's training builds on residual fatigue from the
last, eventually driving the athlete past their Maximum Recoverable Volume (see
`core/volume_landmarks.md`) and into an overreaching or overtraining state.

## Types of Deload

### Volume Deload (most common)
Maintain working intensity (load on bar stays near normal working weights), but reduce
total sets per muscle group by **40–60%**. This keeps the nervous system familiar with
heavy loads while dramatically reducing cumulative fatigue.

*Best for*: strength-focused programs; preserves 1RM readiness.

### Intensity Deload
Maintain training volume (set count), but reduce load to **50–70%** of normal working
weights. Higher rep counts are typical. Joints and connective tissue recover well under
this approach.

*Best for*: hypertrophy phases; cardiovascular and metabolic conditioning is preserved.

### Complete Rest
All training suspended. Rarely optimal beyond 1–2 days; longer periods risk loss of
motor patterns, muscle mass, and psychological disruption. Reserved for acute injury or
illness.

## When to Deload

### Planned Deloads (scheduled)
Insert after a fixed number of hard training weeks — typically every **4–6 weeks** for
intermediate trainees, **3–4 weeks** for advanced trainees. This is the standard approach
in program design (e.g., the fourth week of 5/3/1 is a deload week by design).

Advantages: predictability, easy to track, avoids waiting until fatigue becomes problematic.

### Reactive Deloads (symptom-triggered)
A deload taken in response to observable indicators rather than on a fixed schedule.
Appropriate when self-monitoring is accurate enough to distinguish normal training
fatigue from excessive accumulation.

**Deload indicators**:
- Performance decline across 2+ consecutive sessions on the same lift
- Persistent joint tenderness that does not resolve in 48 hours
- Motivational loss — dread of training rather than normal pre-session inertia
- Sleep quality degradation without other explanation
- DOMS that does not clear between sessions

### In Practice

Most intermediate athletes benefit from planned deloads. Advanced athletes with
well-developed self-monitoring can use a hybrid: schedule a deload window every 4–6 weeks,
but trigger it early if reactive indicators appear sooner.

## How to Structure a Deload Week

**Volume deload template** (recommended for most):

| Session variable | Working week | Deload week |
|-----------------|-------------|------------|
| Sets per muscle group | 100% | 40–60% |
| Load per set | 100% | 90–100% |
| RIR target | 1–3 | 4–6 |
| Session duration | Normal | Shorter |

Keep the exercise selection identical. Maintain frequency if possible — skipping the
gym entirely for a week is not a deload, it is rest.

## What a Deload Is Not

- **Deload ≠ rest week**: complete rest is rarely optimal and is distinct from a deload
- **Deload ≠ weakness**: not deloading is how overtraining occurs; the deload is the
  mechanism that lets accumulated fitness be expressed
- **Deload ≠ always volume reduction**: an intensity deload is equally valid and may be
  preferable in some contexts

## How Systems Differ

Powerlifting programs typically use volume deloads before competition to maintain
bar weight exposure (readiness) while clearing fatigue. 5/3/1 has a built-in deload week
(week 4: 40%–50%–60% sets of 5) every cycle.
Bodybuilding programs may use either type; intensity deloads are common because they
preserve the metabolic and volume adaptations of the preceding block.
Olympic weightlifting uses tapers that are extended deloads — volume drops sharply 1–3
weeks before competition while intensity remains very high, peaking the nervous system.
CrossFit's constantly varied structure provides some built-in recovery; structured deloads
are less formally programmed but still recommended every 4–6 weeks during periods of
structured training.
