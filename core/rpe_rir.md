# RPE and RIR

RPE (Rate of Perceived Exertion) and RIR (Reps in Reserve) are two related tools for
regulating training intensity based on effort rather than a fixed percentage of 1RM.
They are the primary instruments of **autoregulation** — adjusting load on any given day
to reflect actual readiness rather than a pre-planned number that may be too heavy or too
light depending on sleep, stress, and accumulated fatigue.

## Definitions

**RIR — Reps in Reserve**
The number of additional reps you could have completed before reaching technical failure.
RIR 2 means you stopped a set with 2 more reps still available. RIR 0 means the next rep
would have failed.

**RPE — Rate of Perceived Exertion (resistance training scale)**
A 1–10 scale adapted for resistance training by Mike Tuchscherer (Reactive Training Systems).
The direct mapping to RIR:

| RPE | RIR | Description |
|-----|-----|-------------|
| 10  | 0   | Maximal effort — could not do another rep |
| 9   | 1   | One rep remaining |
| 8   | 2   | Two reps remaining — hard set, not a grinder |
| 7   | 3   | Three reps remaining — challenging, still speed in the bar |
| 6   | 4   | Four reps remaining — moderate; bar moves quickly |
| ≤5  | 5+  | Warm-up territory for trained athletes |

The formula: **RPE = 10 − RIR**

## Why RIR Is the More Intuitive Unit

"Two reps left in the tank" is a concrete internal sensation that most trained athletes
can identify. "RPE 8" requires translating a number back into that sensation. Both convey
the same information; RIR is typically easier to communicate and teach, especially for
athletes unfamiliar with Tuchscherer's original system.

A 2016 RCT (Zourdos et al.) validated the RIR-based RPE scale against actual performance
in trained athletes. Intraclass correlation was high in trained subjects — meaning the
self-reported RIR closely matched true remaining capacity. Accuracy was substantially lower
in untrained individuals and in very high rep ranges (15+), where effort perception
becomes less reliable.

## What Autoregulation Solves

Fixed percentage programming assumes your 1RM is stable day-to-day. It is not. Strength
can vary 5–10% between a well-recovered and a fatigued session. If your program prescribes
3×5 at 85% 1RM on a day when your real 1RM is running 8% lower than tested, you are
actually training at ~93% — far harder than intended, accumulating excess fatigue.

RPE/RIR-based programming avoids this by anchoring to effort:
- "Work up to a set of 5 at RPE 8" means the same relative difficulty regardless of the
  day's absolute strength level.
- The load adjusts automatically to the athlete's actual readiness.

## When to Use RPE vs. Fixed Percentages

Both have a place:

| Scenario | Recommended approach |
|----------|---------------------|
| Peaking / competition prep | Fixed percentages (predictability is priority) |
| Volume accumulation blocks | RPE/RIR (daily readiness varies; effort target is stable) |
| Beginners | Fixed percentages (RPE accuracy requires training history to calibrate) |
| Advanced athletes | RPE/RIR or a hybrid (percentages set the target; RPE guides daily adjustment) |
| Programs like 5/3/1 | Hybrid — percentages for main sets, AMRAP provides RIR data |

## How Systems Differ

Powerlifting programs with a competition date use percentages in the peaking block for
predictability. Volume phases increasingly use RPE to manage fatigue without testing a
true 1RM every cycle. Bodybuilding programs use RIR almost exclusively — proximity to
failure (0–3 RIR) is the key hypertrophy stimulus, and the absolute load is secondary.
Olympic weightlifting rarely uses RPE because technical failure (position breakdown) occurs
before muscular failure, making RPE a poor proxy for actual training stress in that domain.
