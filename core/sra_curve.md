# SRA Curve

The SRA (Stimulus–Recovery–Adaptation) curve describes the fatigue, recovery, and
supercompensation a body goes through after a single training stimulus. It is the
mechanistic foundation of why all periodized programs work — and why programs that
ignore recovery timing produce stagnation or overtraining.

## Historical Context: GAS vs. SRA

Early periodization theory borrowed Hans Selye's **General Adaptation Syndrome (GAS)**
as its conceptual foundation. Selye's original work, however, was based on systemic,
lethal-level pharmacological stressors — not the localized, sub-maximal mechanical
stress of resistance training. Buckner et al. (2017) demonstrated that GAS models
systemic non-specific responses poorly suited to the localized tissue microtrauma and
neuromuscular adaptations that resistance training induces.

The SRA framework (derived from GAS but applied correctly) addresses this by modeling
**localized depletion and supercompensation** of specific tissues — muscle fiber proteins,
glycogen stores, connective tissue — rather than a systemic alarm response. Cunanan et al.
(2018) formalized the connection: the Alarm, Resistance, and Exhaustion stages of GAS
correspond broadly to the Stimulus, Recovery, and (if training load exceeds recoverable
capacity) Overtraining phases of SRA — but the practical application requires treating
each muscle group's SRA curve as independent.

## Core Definition

Training lowers functional performance in the moment (fatigue phase), which then rebounds
during recovery and, in the supercompensation phase, rises above the original baseline.
The best time for the next session falls at the **peak of supercompensation** — not while
fatigue is still present, and not after supercompensation has decayed back to baseline.

Miss the window too early: training on residual fatigue accumulates damage faster than
adaptation. Miss it too late: the supercompensation decays and each session returns to
baseline rather than building on a higher floor.

## Three Phases

1. **Stimulus** — training causes muscular microtrauma and metabolic stress; performance
   temporarily drops below baseline; the alarm phase in GAS terms
2. **Recovery** — nutrition and sleep drive repair; inflammatory resolution clears the
   acute damage signal; performance rebounds toward baseline
3. **Adaptation** — supercompensation sets a new baseline slightly above the previous
   one; this is when the fitness gain is expressed

A fourth state — **exhaustion** (GAS) / **non-functional overreaching** or **overtraining**
(sports science) — occurs when repeated stimuli are applied without adequate recovery,
driving performance below baseline for weeks or months.

## Curve Length and Training Volume

The SRA cycle length is determined by the magnitude of the stimulus:

| Stimulus magnitude | Approximate SRA cycle | Example |
|-------------------|----------------------|---------|
| Low (single small-muscle isolation) | 24–36 h | Cable curl, lateral raise |
| Moderate (compound moderate volume) | 48–72 h | Moderate-volume squat session |
| High (high-volume large-muscle compound) | 72–96 h or more | Heavy high-volume deadlift session |

These ranges are approximate and vary significantly by training status. Novices have
longer SRA cycles because their damage response is proportionally greater relative to
their recovery capacity. Advanced athletes have shorter cycles because their recovery
infrastructure (hormonal environment, local tissue resilience) is more developed.

This directly determines **training frequency design**: a muscle group trained with high
volume requires more days before the next stimulus than one trained with low volume.
Frequency and volume are co-determined by the SRA curve, not independently set.

## Interaction with Volume Landmarks

Volume accumulation across a mesocycle gradually extends the SRA curve because cumulative
fatigue adds to the acute fatigue of each session. This is why performance often declines
in the final weeks of a hard training block even when per-session stimulus is unchanged —
the accumulated fatigue layer lengthens effective SRA cycle duration beyond the available
recovery window. A deload resets this accumulated fatigue, allowing supercompensation to
be expressed.

## How Systems Differ

Each system arranges different stimulus density and recovery intervals to suit its goals.
Those differences are recorded in each system's `index.md`; this entry defines only the
principle itself.

- **Powerlifting**: Frequency is calibrated so that the next heavy session falls at or
  near supercompensation peak for the competition lifts. This typically means 2–3x/week
  per lift at submaximal loads, with the highest-intensity session occurring when fatigue
  from prior sessions has fully cleared.
- **Bodybuilding**: Higher volume per session extends SRA cycles. Training frequency is
  typically 1–2x/week per muscle group, with the second session placed when soreness and
  performance have returned to baseline.
- **Olympic Weightlifting**: Daily or twice-daily training is possible because session
  volumes are often low per movement; the SRA curve for a single lift at low volume
  resolves in 24 hours, allowing high frequency.
- **CrossFit**: The mixed-modal structure makes SRA modeling complex; glycogen SRA and
  muscular SRA operate on different timescales. A 48-hour recovery window between
  high-intensity sessions is the minimum practical rule.
