# Olympic Weightlifting

Olympic weightlifting is the sport of lifting the heaviest possible barbell from the floor to overhead in two competition movements: the snatch (one continuous movement) and the clean & jerk (two-phase lift). It demands the highest expression of explosive power, coordination, and positional precision of any barbell sport.

## Defining Principles

- **Technique is the ceiling**: Unlike powerlifting, technical error — not strength — is the primary limiter in most athletes for most of their career. Positions must be learned before load can be added.
- **Explosive force application**: Both competition lifts require maximum rate of force development, not sustained grinding effort. Training emphasizes speed, timing, and bar path consistency.
- **High frequency, moderate volume per session**: Because the lifts are technical skills, frequent exposure (often 4–6 sessions per week) at manageable intensities builds skill faster than infrequent high-volume sessions.
- **Position work and pulling derivatives**: Heavy pulling movements (clean pull, snatch pull, deadlift variations) and positional work (overhead squats, front squats) make up the majority of non-competition-lift volume.
- **Peaking for competition**: Like powerlifting, training cycles build toward a competition max. The final weeks reduce volume sharply and increase intensity to allow full neural recovery.

## What Distinguishes Olympic Weightlifting from Other Systems

The two competition movements are the most technically complex barbell lifts in common use. Athletes may spend years on technique before strength becomes the limiter. Unlike powerlifting, ballistic force application is required — the bar must be accelerated through the full pull and caught in a specific receiving position. Unlike CrossFit, the Olympic lifts are trained as pure skill-strength movements, not mixed into metabolic conditioning.
